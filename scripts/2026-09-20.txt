Parashah (Rashi): Sukkot I (Leviticus 22:26-23:44)
Date: 2026-09-20

Welcome back to Rashi in Brief, the podcast where each week we explore the Torah portion through the brilliant, incisive eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator. This week we're looking at the special Torah reading for the first day of Sukkot, drawn from Leviticus chapters twenty-two and twenty-three. It's a rich and dense portion, and Rashi has a lot to say, so let's dive in.

<break time="1.5s" />

This Torah reading covers some truly foundational ground. It begins with laws about the fitness of animal sacrifices, including the minimum age of an animal before it can be offered, and the prohibition against slaughtering a mother animal and its young on the same day. Then it moves into one of the great chapters of the Torah: Leviticus twenty-three, which lays out the entire calendar of Jewish festivals, from Shabbat to Passover, from the counting of the Omer to Shavuot, from Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, and finally to Sukkot itself. It's essentially God's appointment book with the Jewish people. What's fascinating about Rashi's approach here is how many of his comments address technical legal questions: what exactly does a word mean, what is included or excluded by a particular phrase, and how do we reconcile apparent repetitions or contradictions in the text. But alongside these precise legal observations, Rashi also draws out moments of stunning emotional and spiritual depth, reminding us that behind the laws there is a living relationship between God and Israel.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let me walk you through some of Rashi's most striking comments on this portion. We'll start near the beginning, with the law found in Leviticus twenty-two, verse twenty-eight, which prohibits slaughtering an animal and its young on the same day. The Torah uses the phrase oto v'et b'no, literally "it and its son," using a masculine pronoun, oto. Rashi immediately notices something odd. If the law were about the father animal, the masculine pronoun would make perfect sense. But Rashi tells us, based on the Talmud, that this law actually applies only to the mother animal and its offspring. You may not slaughter the dam and her young on the same day, but a father animal and its offspring may be slaughtered on the same day. Why? Because with a mother, the parentage is certain, while with a father, it often isn't. So Rashi is teaching us that the Torah sometimes uses a grammatical form that doesn't match the legal reality, and a careful reader needs to know when the text is speaking loosely. It's a perfect example of how Rashi reads every word, even a single pronoun, as carrying legal significance.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's jump ahead to the festival calendar. In Leviticus twenty-three, verse three, the Torah mentions Shabbat right before listing all the holidays. Rashi asks a great question: what does Shabbat have to do with the festivals? Shabbat is fixed, it comes every seven days no matter what. The festivals, by contrast, were historically dependent on the Jewish court, the Sanhedrin, which proclaimed the new months and determined when the holidays would fall. So why does the Torah place them side by side? Rashi answers: to teach that whoever desecrates the festivals is considered as if they desecrated Shabbat, and whoever properly observes the festivals is considered as if they kept Shabbat. In other words, don't think of the festivals as somehow lesser than Shabbat. They carry the same weight and sanctity. It's a powerful reminder that all of sacred time, whether divinely fixed or humanly proclaimed, deserves our full reverence.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of the most emotionally stirring passages in this portion is Leviticus twenty-two, verses thirty-two and thirty-three, where God says, "You shall not profane My holy name, and I shall be sanctified among the children of Israel." Rashi reads the first part, the prohibition against profaning God's name, as referring to deliberate transgression. But then he asks: if we already know not to profane God's name, what does the positive command to sanctify God's name add? He answers that it introduces the concept of kiddush Hashem, the sanctification of God's name, which in its ultimate form means being willing to give one's life rather than violate certain core commandments. But Rashi adds a remarkable psychological insight. He says that a person who surrenders to martyrdom must do so with genuine willingness to die, not with the secret expectation that God will perform a miracle and save them. He brings the example of Chananyah, Mishael, and Azaryah from the Book of Daniel, who were thrown into a fiery furnace and declared to the king: whether God saves us or not, we will not worship your gods. That is the standard. True sanctification of God's name means acting without conditions.

<break time="0.75s" />

Let's turn now to Sukkot itself. In verse forty, the Torah commands us to take the fruit of a beautiful tree, a branch of palm trees, boughs of thick-leaved trees, and willows of the brook. Rashi zeroes in on the phrase p'ri eitz hadar, the fruit of the beautiful tree. How do we know this refers to the etrog, the citron? Rashi offers a beautiful wordplay. He explains that hadar can be read as ha-dar, meaning "that which dwells," referring to a tree whose fruit remains dwelling on the tree from year to year, through all the seasons. Unlike most fruit trees where the fruit ripens and falls, the etrog stays on the branch across multiple years. That's how we identify it. It's a lovely example of Rashi using etymology and agricultural observation to unlock the meaning of a biblical phrase.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, and perhaps most beautifully for the holiday of Sukkot, Rashi comments on the very last verse of our reading, Leviticus twenty-three, verse forty-three, where the Torah explains the reason for the commandment of dwelling in sukkot, in booths: "For I made the children of Israel dwell in sukkot when I brought them out of Egypt." Rashi tells us that these sukkot were not literal huts or booths. They were the Clouds of Glory, the ananei ha-kavod, the divine clouds that surrounded and protected the Israelites during their forty years in the wilderness. When we sit in the sukkah, we're not just commemorating primitive desert shelters. We're reenacting the experience of being enveloped in God's protective presence. The fragile walls and leafy roof of the sukkah are meant to remind us that our true shelter is not brick and mortar but the embrace of the Divine.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, a few themes emerge in Rashi's treatment of this portion. First, there is his relentless precision. Every pronoun, every unusual spelling, every seemingly redundant phrase is a doorway into deeper meaning. Rashi never lets the text just wash over him. Second, there is a recurring theme of intention and sincerity. Whether it's the intention behind a sacrifice, the willingness behind martyrdom, or the awareness behind observing a festival, Rashi insists that what matters is not just the outward act but the inner posture. Third, and most movingly for Sukkot, Rashi reminds us that Jewish observance is ultimately about relationship. The festivals are not mere rituals but appointments with the Divine. The sukkah is not just a structure but a memory of God's sheltering love. And even the seemingly technical laws about sacrifices point toward a God who is, as Rashi says, faithful to pay the reward.
