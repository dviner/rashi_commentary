Parashah (Rashi): Vaetchanan (Deuteronomy 3:23-7:11)
Date: 2026-07-24

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week's portion is Vaetchanan, and it is one of the richest and most emotionally powerful sections in the entire Torah. Let's dive in and see what Rashi reveals.

<break time="1.5s" />

Vaetchanan, which means "and I implored," opens with Moses pleading with God to let him enter the Promised Land, a plea that God ultimately denies. From there, Moses turns to the people of Israel and delivers some of the most foundational passages in all of Jewish life: a retelling of the giving of the Torah at Sinai, a restatement of the Ten Commandments, and the Shema, the declaration of God's oneness that Jews recite morning and evening to this day. He also warns the people against idolatry, urges them to remember what they witnessed at Sinai, and calls on them to love God with everything they have. It is a portion that moves between deep personal anguish and soaring communal vision. Rashi, throughout this portion, is drawn to the precise language Moses uses, the emotional subtext beneath the words, and the theological implications of small grammatical choices. He is especially attentive to moments where a word seems unnecessary, or where the plain meaning doesn't quite capture the full depth of what's happening.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with the very first word of the portion: Vaetchanan, "and I implored." Moses could have used any of the ten Hebrew words for prayer, so why this one? Rashi explains that the root of this word, chen, means grace, an undeserved gift. Even though Moses had a lifetime of righteous deeds he could have pointed to, he didn't ask God to reward him for his merits. Instead, he asked for a free gift, an act of pure divine generosity. Rashi is teaching us something profound about the nature of prayer and humility: even the greatest human being who ever lived approached God not as someone owed a debt, but as someone asking for kindness.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at Moses's plea itself. He says to God, "You have begun to show Your servant Your greatness and Your strong hand." This sounds like a compliment, but Rashi reads it as a carefully crafted argument. Moses is saying: You opened the door to prayer. Back at the golden calf, God told Moses, "Leave Me alone, and I will destroy them." But Moses understood that God was hinting, "If you pray for them, I might relent." So Moses prayed, and God relented. Now Moses is using the same logic: You taught me that prayer can change decrees. You started this. Let me try again. It is a remarkably bold and intimate portrait of the relationship between Moses and God, one where Moses essentially says, "I learned this move from You."

<break time="0.75s" />

God's response to Moses is devastating in its brevity: Rav lach, which literally means "it is enough for you" or "you have plenty." Rashi offers two readings, and together they paint a complete picture. First, the refusal: Stop praying, God says, so that people won't say the Master is harsh and the student is stubborn and importunate. There is a dignity to be preserved on both sides. But then Rashi gives a second, gentler reading: Rav lach, there is much reserved for you. Much goodness is stored up for you in the World to Come. In other words, God is not rejecting Moses; He is redirecting him. What awaits you is greater than what you're asking for. This double reading is classic Rashi, holding both the pain and the consolation in a single phrase.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving deeper into the portion, we come to the Shema: "Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One." Rashi's comment here is breathtaking in its scope. He explains that when we say "the Lord our God," we mean that right now, God is recognized as our God, the God of Israel, but not yet acknowledged by the other nations of the world. And when we say "the Lord is One," we are expressing a future hope: that one day, God will be recognized as the one God by all of humanity, as the prophet Zechariah says, "On that day the Lord shall be One and His name One." So every time a Jew recites the Shema, they are not only declaring a fact about the present but voicing a vision of the future. Rashi turns six familiar Hebrew words into a statement of both theology and eschatology.

<break time="0.75s" />

Following the Shema, the Torah commands, "And you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might." Rashi unpacks each phrase with remarkable precision. On "with all your heart," he notes the unusual Hebrew spelling, levavcha, with two letters bet instead of the expected one. This hints, Rashi says, that you should love God with both of your inclinations, the good inclination and the evil inclination, meaning even your desires and drives that pull you away from goodness should be channeled toward serving God. On "with all your soul," Rashi says simply: even if He takes your soul, even if loving God requires martyrdom. And on "with all your might," Rashi offers two readings: with all your money, because some people value their possessions even more than their lives, and also with whatever measure God metes out to you, whether good or painful. Love God in prosperity and in suffering alike. As David said in Psalms, whether I lift the cup of salvation or find trouble and sorrow, I call upon the name of the Lord.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, Rashi makes a beautiful comment on the command to teach Torah diligently, veshinantam, to your children. He explains that the word comes from a root meaning to sharpen, meaning the words of Torah should be so sharp and familiar in your mouth that if someone asks you a question, you don't stammer or hesitate, but answer immediately. And then he adds something surprising: the word "children" here doesn't only mean your biological sons and daughters. It also means your students. Throughout the tradition, Rashi says, disciples are called children, and teachers are called parents. When the prophet Elisha cried out to his teacher Elijah, "My father, my father," he was using the language of this very relationship. Teaching Torah is an act of spiritual parenthood.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, several themes emerge that define Rashi's reading of Vaetchanan. One is the theme of humility before God. Moses, the greatest prophet, asks for grace, not justice. Even his bold arguments are framed with deference. A second theme is the power and limits of prayer. Moses models passionate, reasoned prayer, and God's response teaches that sometimes the answer is no, but that no is wrapped in love. A third theme is the idea that Torah must be internalized so deeply that it becomes part of who you are, sharp on your tongue, woven into every moment of lying down and rising up. And throughout, Rashi shows us that single words, even single letters, carry enormous meaning. A doubled letter in the word for heart reveals a whole theology of the human soul. A verb tense teaches us about leadership. Rashi's method reminds us that the Torah rewards the closest possible attention, and that the surface of the text is only the beginning.
