Parashah (Rashi): Nitzavim-Vayeilech (Deuteronomy 29:9-31:30)
Date: 2026-08-30

Welcome back to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week we have a double portion called Nitzavim-Vayeilech, found in Deuteronomy chapters twenty-nine through thirty-one, and Rashi has some truly remarkable things to say about these final chapters of Moses's life.

<break time="1.5s" />

This double portion takes place on the very last day of Moses's life. He gathers the entire people of Israel, from the leaders down to the woodcutters and water drawers, and brings them into a covenant with God. He warns them about the consequences of idolatry, but he also offers them extraordinary hope, promising that no matter how far they stray, return is always possible. He then speaks about the accessibility of the Torah itself, famously declaring that it is not in heaven or across the sea but right here, close to us. In the second portion, Vayeilech, Moses formally transfers leadership to Joshua, instructs the people about a public Torah reading every seven years, and begins preparing them for a future without him. Throughout these chapters, Rashi is drawn to subtle word choices, grammatical oddities, and juxtapositions between sections. He asks why certain passages appear where they do, what unusual verb forms really mean, and how small details reveal large truths about God's relationship with Israel.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with the very opening words of the portion. The Torah says, "You are standing this day, all of you, before the Lord your God." Rashi asks a question that might not occur to us at first: why does the Torah need to say "this day"? And why is this passage placed right after the terrifying curses of the previous chapter? Rashi offers a beautiful answer. He says that when Israel heard those ninety-eight curses, their faces turned pale and they cried out, "Who can possibly withstand all of this?" So Moses began to calm them, saying in effect, "Look, you are standing here today! You have provoked God many times, and yet He has not destroyed you. You are still here." And then Rashi adds something poetic about the word "today," saying that the day is like Israel's existence: though it grows dark for a time, it shines again. The very curses and sufferings, paradoxically, are what give Israel their permanence and stability. It is a stunning reframing: what looked like a terrifying threat becomes a source of reassurance.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at a phrase that seems like a minor detail but opens up something profound. The Torah lists all the people present at this covenant ceremony, ending with "from the woodcutter to the drawer of water." Why mention these humble laborers specifically? Rashi tells us this teaches that Canaanites came to Moses wanting to convert, just as the Gibeonites later came to Joshua through trickery. Moses accepted them but assigned them these roles as woodcutters and water drawers. What is remarkable here is the inclusiveness of the covenant: even these newcomers, people from outside the community, are standing before God and entering the covenant. And the Torah goes on to say that this covenant is made not only with those present but also with those "who are not here today." Rashi clarifies that this does not mean people who happened to be absent. Rather, it refers to all future generations. Every Jew who would ever live is included in this moment. The covenant is not just a historical event but an eternal bond.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of the most famous verses in the Torah appears in this portion: "It is not in heaven." Moses is speaking about the Torah and its commandments, and he says, "This commandment is not too wondrous for you, nor is it far off. It is not in heaven... nor is it beyond the sea... but the word is very near to you." Rashi makes two incisive comments here. First, he explains that the word usually translated as "too wondrous" or "too difficult," niflet, actually means "concealed" or "hidden." The Torah is not hidden from you. And then, with characteristic boldness, Rashi adds: even if it were in heaven, it would still be your duty to go up after it and learn it. The accessibility of Torah is not an excuse for complacency. It is a gift, but it also comes with an expectation: you have been given the Torah in writing and orally, so you have no excuse not to engage with it.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving into Vayeilech, the Torah tells us, "And Moses went and spoke these words to all Israel. I am one hundred and twenty years old today. I can no longer go out and come in." Rashi is troubled. You might think Moses is saying his body has grown weak, but the Torah itself tells us later that when Moses died, "his eye was not dim nor his natural force abated." So what does he mean? Rashi offers two explanations. First, Moses means he is no longer permitted to lead, because authority has been transferred to Joshua. And second, Rashi says something deeply moving: Moses can no longer "go out and come in" in matters of Torah, because the traditions and wellsprings of wisdom were stopped up for him. Even for Moses, the greatest prophet, the end of his mission meant the closing of channels of insight. This is not physical decline. It is the recognition that his role is complete.

<break time="0.75s" />

There is a fascinating contrast Rashi draws between what Moses says to Joshua and what God says to Joshua. Moses tells Joshua, "You shall go with this people into the land." But God later tells Joshua, "You shall bring the children of Israel into the land." Rashi explains the difference with striking directness. Moses is saying: the elders of the generation will be with you; lead collaboratively, following their counsel. But God is saying something very different: you shall bring them even against their will. If necessary, take a stick and beat them over the head. There can be only one leader for a generation, not two. This tension between collaborative and decisive leadership is left standing in the text, and Rashi lets us feel its full force.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, there is a poignant comment near the end. Moses says, "After my death you will utterly corrupt yourselves." But we know from the book of Judges that the people actually served God faithfully throughout the days of Joshua. So how could Moses say corruption would follow immediately after his death? Rashi's answer is tender: a person's student should be as dear to him as his own self. As long as Joshua lived, it was as though Moses himself were still alive. When Moses said "after my death," he was including Joshua's lifetime as an extension of his own.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, several themes run through Rashi's commentary on this double portion. There is the theme of reassurance: even after devastating curses, God's relationship with Israel endures. There is the theme of accessibility: Torah is not hidden or distant but given directly into human hands. There is the theme of leadership and transition: what it means to pass the mantle, and how different styles of leadership serve different moments. And there is the theme of covenant as eternal: binding not just those who stood at that assembly but every generation to come. Throughout it all, Rashi shows us his characteristic method: he notices a word that seems unnecessary, a phrase that could have been written differently, a passage placed in an unexpected location, and from these small observations he draws out meanings that are at once precise and expansive. He teaches us that careful reading is itself an act of devotion.
