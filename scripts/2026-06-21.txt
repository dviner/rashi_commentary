Parashah (Rashi): Chukat-Balak (Numbers 19:1-25:9)
Date: 2026-06-21

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, our weekly journey through the Torah portion with the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known simply as Rashi. This week we have a double portion called Chukat-Balak, covering Numbers chapters nineteen through twenty-five, and as always, we'll be exploring how Rashi's sharp eye for textual detail opens up layers of meaning we might otherwise miss.

<break time="1.5s" />

This is an enormous double portion packed with dramatic events. Chukat begins with the mysterious ritual of the red cow, whose ashes are used to purify people who have become ritually impure through contact with the dead. We then witness the death of Miriam and the subsequent water crisis, leading to the fateful moment when Moses strikes the rock instead of speaking to it, a sin that costs him entry into the Promised Land. Aaron too dies atop Mount Hor. The people face serpent attacks, military conflicts, and must navigate around hostile Edom and Moab. In the Balak section, the Moabite king Balak hires the prophet Balaam to curse Israel, but every attempt at a curse is transformed into a blessing. Rashi, throughout this portion, is drawn to several kinds of questions. He asks why specific rituals seem to defy logic, why certain details appear in unexpected places, and what the juxtaposition of stories teaches us about hidden connections between events.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with the very first words of the portion. The Torah says, "This is the ordinance of the law," introducing the red cow ritual, and Rashi immediately notices that the Hebrew word used here is chukah, meaning a decree or statute. Why this particular word? Rashi explains that because Satan and the nations of the world taunt Israel by asking what possible reason there could be for this strange commandment, Scripture deliberately uses the term chukah to signal that this is an enactment from God that transcends human understanding. You have no right to criticize it. This sets the tone for the entire opening section. Some commandments, Rashi is telling us, are meant to be obeyed not because we fully grasp them, but because we trust the One who commanded them. And yet, fascinatingly, Rashi then goes on to offer a beautiful midrashic explanation of every detail of the red cow, connecting each element to the sin of the golden calf, showing that even a chukah can carry deep symbolic meaning.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at what happens immediately after the red cow section. The Torah tells us that Miriam died and was buried, and then, in the very next verse, it says there was no water for the congregation. Rashi asks the obvious question: why are these two facts placed side by side? His answer is elegant. From this juxtaposition we learn that during the entire forty years in the wilderness, the Israelites had their miraculous well of water through the merit of Miriam. When she died, the well disappeared. This is one of the most beloved teachings in all of Rashi's commentary, and it illustrates a principle he returns to again and again. The Torah's arrangement of material is never accidental. The placement of Miriam's death right before the water crisis is the Torah's way of honoring her legacy and crediting her righteousness with sustaining an entire nation. Rashi also connects the death of Miriam to the red cow section by noting that just as sacrifices effect atonement, so too does the death of the righteous effect atonement for their generation.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now we come to one of the most consequential moments in the entire Torah: Moses striking the rock. God had told Moses to speak to the rock, but instead Moses struck it twice. Rashi digs into why Moses struck the rock at all. He explains that the original rock from which water had flowed during those forty years had vanished after Miriam's death. Moses and Aaron spoke to a different rock and nothing happened. They assumed they needed to strike it, as God had commanded on a previous occasion back at the beginning of the wilderness journey. So Moses struck, and water came, but only after a second blow. The real tragedy, Rashi explains, is what was lost. Had Moses spoken to the rock and it obeyed, the entire congregation would have witnessed an extraordinary lesson. They would have said, if this rock, which cannot speak or hear, fulfills God's word, how much more should we? The missed opportunity for sanctifying God's name publicly is what made this sin so severe. Rashi adds a remarkable point: Moses had shown doubt before, questioning God privately about whether there would be enough meat for the people, but because that was said in private, God spared him. Here, before all of Israel, Scripture does not spare him, because of what was at stake for the sanctification of God's name.

<break time="0.75s" />

The death of Aaron is described with extraordinary tenderness in Rashi's commentary. God tells Moses to take Aaron up to Mount Hor, and Rashi says the word take here means to draw him with consoling words. Moses is to say to Aaron, "Happy are you that you will see your crown given to your son, a privilege that I myself will not have." Then Rashi paints an intimate scene that appears nowhere in the plain text. Moses told Aaron to enter the cave, and he entered. He saw a bed already prepared and a light burning. Moses said, stretch out your hands, and he did. Close your mouth, and he did. Close your eyes, and he did. And Aaron's soul departed in perfect peace. Rashi tells us that Moses longed for that very same gentle death, which is why Scripture later says Moses died "as Aaron your brother died," meaning, the death you wished for yourself. This is Rashi at his most poignant, filling in silences in the text to reveal the deep love between brothers and the spiritual beauty of a righteous death.

<break time="0.75s" />

When the people are later bitten by fiery serpents as punishment for complaining, God tells Moses to fashion a serpent and place it on a pole. Anyone who was bitten could look at it and live. Rashi asks the obvious question: could a copper serpent really cause death or life? And he answers with a teaching from the Talmud. When the Israelites looked upward at the serpent on the pole and thereby directed their hearts to their Father in Heaven, they were healed. If they did not do this, they wasted away. The copper serpent was not magic. It was a vehicle for turning the people's attention back to God. Rashi also notices something else. The Torah never actually told Moses to make the serpent out of copper. God simply said to make a serpent, using the Hebrew word nachash. Moses chose copper, nechoshet, because the words sound alike. One term fitting the other term, Rashi says, revealing Moses's creativity even within the boundaries of divine instruction.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, I want to highlight Rashi's comment on the aftermath of Aaron's death and the Canaanite attack. The Torah says the Canaanite king heard and attacked Israel. But Rashi tells us this was actually Amalek in disguise. The Amalekites changed their language to speak Canaanite so that Israel would pray for victory over the wrong enemy. But Israel noticed that though the language was Canaanite, the clothing was Amalekite. So rather than specifying any nation in their prayer, they simply said, "God, deliver this people into our hands," praying in general terms to outmaneuver the deception. This is a wonderful example of Rashi drawing on midrashic tradition to explain puzzling textual details, turning what seems like a straightforward military report into a story about spiritual discernment and the power of prayer.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, several themes emerge from Rashi's approach to this portion. One is the idea that proximity in the text reveals causation. Miriam's death causes the water crisis; Aaron's death causes the disappearance of the protective clouds and opens the door to attack. Another theme is the cost of public versus private failure. Moses's private doubt was forgiven, but his public misstep at the rock was not, because leadership carries a responsibility to sanctify God's name before the people. There is also a deep thread about humility running through the portion, from the cedar wood and hyssop of the red cow ritual, symbolizing that the proud person must lower himself like the lowly hyssop to find atonement, to Aaron's gentle death, which reflected a life spent pursuing peace. Rashi consistently shows us that the Torah is not merely recording history. It is teaching us how to live, how to lead, and how to relate to God, through every word choice, every juxtaposition, and every silence.
