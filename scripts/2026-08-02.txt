Parashah (Rashi): Re'eh (Deuteronomy 11:26-16:17)
Date: 2026-08-02

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rashi. This week's portion is called Re'eh, which means "Behold" or "See," and it's found in the book of Deuteronomy, chapters eleven through sixteen. Let's dive in and see what Rashi reveals about this rich and complex portion.

<break time="1.5s" />

Re'eh is one of those portions that covers an enormous amount of ground. Moses is speaking to the Israelites as they stand on the edge of the Promised Land, and he lays before them a dramatic choice: blessing and curse. If they follow God's commandments, blessing; if they turn away, curse. From there, the portion moves into detailed laws about centralizing worship in one chosen place, destroying idolatry, the rules around eating meat, tithes and offerings, the false prophet, the person who tries to entice you to worship other gods, and the festivals. It's really a blueprint for how the Israelites are to live once they enter the land. Rashi, as always, is drawn to the small details that reveal big ideas. He notices unusual word choices, doubled verbs, and apparent contradictions between verses, and he uses these as doorways into deeper meaning. In this portion especially, Rashi pays close attention to how the Torah distinguishes between different stages of Israel's history, between what's permitted now versus later, and between the ideal and the practical.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start with a comment that seems simple but carries enormous weight. In chapter twelve, verse two, the Torah commands the Israelites to utterly destroy all the places where the nations served their gods. In Hebrew, the verse uses a doubled verb: aved te'abdun, which literally means "destroy, you shall destroy." Rashi seizes on this doubling and says it means: destroy and then destroy again. In other words, when uprooting idolatry, you must be absolutely thorough. You can't just knock down the statue and walk away. You have to remove every last trace. This isn't just a practical instruction. It's Rashi teaching us something about the nature of spiritual danger: half-measures aren't enough when it comes to things that can pull you away from your core values.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at a fascinating comment about the laws of slaughtering animals for food. In chapter twelve, verse twenty-one, the Torah says: "Then thou shalt slaughter as I have commanded thee." Rashi picks up on those words, "as I have commanded thee," and asks a pointed question. Where exactly did God command the specific rules of how to slaughter an animal? If you search the entire written Torah, you won't find detailed instructions for ritual slaughter anywhere. Rashi's answer is striking: this verse is proof that there was an oral tradition, laws communicated to Moses on Mount Sinai that were never written down in the Torah itself. This is one of the most important comments in the entire portion because it establishes a textual basis for the entire concept of the Oral Torah, the vast body of Jewish law and tradition that accompanies the written text. Rashi finds this enormous principle hiding inside just three Hebrew words.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now, here's a comment that touches on something very human. In chapter twelve, verse twenty, the Torah says that when God enlarges your territory, then you may say, "I will eat meat." Rashi draws from this a remarkable lesson about proper conduct. He says the Torah is teaching us derech eretz, the proper way to live: a person should not desire to eat meat unless they live in a state of abundance and wealth. In other words, the Torah is offering guidance not just about what's permitted, but about how to manage desire. Don't reach for luxuries beyond your means. It's a surprisingly modern-sounding piece of financial and ethical wisdom, and Rashi draws it straight from the sequence of the verses.

<break time="0.75s" />

Let's turn to something darker but deeply important. Chapter thirteen discusses the case of a false prophet who performs a sign or a wonder and then says, "Let us go worship other gods." The sign actually comes true, yet the Torah says: do not listen to him. A listener might reasonably ask, well, if the sign came true, doesn't that prove something? Rashi addresses this directly. He says that God allows the false prophet to perform the sign as a test: "For the Lord your God is testing you, to know whether you truly love the Lord your God." This is a profound idea. Truth isn't determined by spectacle. A miracle doesn't validate a message if the message itself contradicts what you know to be right. Rashi is teaching us that loyalty to core principles must be stronger than the dazzle of extraordinary events.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, consider Rashi's comment on the person who secretly entices you to worship idols. The Torah lists, in chapter thirteen, all the people closest to you who might try this: your brother, your mother's son, the wife of your bosom, your friend who is as your own soul. Rashi identifies "your friend who is as your own soul" as referring to your father. He then explains that Scripture deliberately lists those who are dearest to you to make the point unmistakable: if even these beloved people must not be spared when they try to pull you away from God, how much more so a stranger. And Rashi notes that the Torah says this enticement happens "in secret," because that's how seduction typically works, in whispers, in private, in the twilight. There's a deep psychological realism here. Rashi understands that the greatest spiritual threats often don't come from enemies but from those we love and trust, speaking quietly in moments of vulnerability.

<break time="1.5s" />

When we step back and look at Rashi's commentary across this entire portion, a few powerful themes emerge. First, Rashi is deeply concerned with thoroughness and precision. Whether it's uprooting idolatry completely, slaughtering animals according to exact oral traditions, or understanding exactly which sacrifices belong where, Rashi insists that details matter and that carelessness in spiritual life has real consequences. Second, Rashi consistently reveals layers beneath the surface. A doubled verb becomes a legal principle. Three words become proof of an entire oral tradition. A dietary permission becomes a lesson in self-restraint. This is Rashi's great gift: showing us that the Torah operates on multiple levels simultaneously. And third, there is a recurring thread about discernment, about the ability to see through appearances. Don't be fooled by the false prophet's miracle. Don't be swayed by a loved one's whispered enticement. The Torah, as Rashi reads it, is training us not just in obedience but in wisdom, in the capacity to distinguish truth from deception even when deception wears a compelling disguise.
