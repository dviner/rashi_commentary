Parashah (Rashi): Korach (Numbers 16:1-18:32)
Date: 2026-06-14

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rashi. This week we're looking at Parshat Korach, found in the book of Numbers, chapters sixteen through eighteen. It's a dramatic portion, and Rashi has so much to teach us about what lies beneath the surface of this story.

<break time="1.5s" />

So let me set the stage. Parshat Korach tells the story of the most famous rebellion in the Torah. A man named Korach, a Levite from a prominent family, rises up against Moses and Aaron, challenging their authority. He's joined by Dathan and Abiram from the tribe of Reuben, along with two hundred and fifty distinguished leaders of the community. They accuse Moses and Aaron of elevating themselves above the people, and things escalate dramatically — the earth opens up and swallows the rebels, fire consumes the incense-bearers, and a plague strikes the community before Aaron himself stops it with incense. The portion then concludes with God reaffirming Aaron's priesthood through the miracle of his budding staff, and laying out the gifts and responsibilities assigned to the priests and Levites. Rashi, throughout this portion, is deeply interested in the psychology of the characters — what motivated Korach, why Moses responded the way he did, and what subtle details in the text reveal about the dynamics of this conflict.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's dive into some of Rashi's most fascinating comments. The very first word of the portion presents a puzzle. The Torah says "Vayikach Korach," which literally means "and Korach took." But took what? The verse never tells us what he took. Rashi offers two explanations. First, he says Korach "took himself" to one side — he separated himself from the community in order to challenge the priesthood. The word "took" here means he removed himself, the way we might say someone "took off." But Rashi also offers a second reading: that Korach "took" people — he won over the leaders of the Sanhedrin, the great council, with smooth, persuasive words. So in one word, Rashi shows us both the isolation of the rebel and the seductive power of his rhetoric.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, Rashi notices something curious about the genealogy. The Torah traces Korach's lineage — son of Izhar, son of Kohath, son of Levi — but stops there. It doesn't say "son of Jacob," even though Levi was Jacob's son. Why the omission? Rashi explains that Jacob himself prayed that his name would not be associated with Korach's rebellion. And Rashi points us to the verse in Genesis where Jacob says, "With their assembly, let my honor not be united." So Jacob's name is deliberately left out here. But Rashi adds a beautiful counterpoint: Jacob's name does appear in connection with Korach's descendants in the book of Chronicles — but only when they are listed as singers and musicians in the Temple. Jacob wanted his name linked to the worthy legacy that would eventually emerge from this family, not to the rebellion itself.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now, what actually motivated Korach? Rashi gives us a remarkably detailed psychological portrait. Korach was the son of Izhar, the second son of Kohath. He felt that since Amram, the eldest brother, had produced Moses the king and Aaron the High Priest, the next position of leadership should go to Izhar's son — that is, to himself. But instead, Moses appointed Elzaphan, the son of Uziel, the youngest brother, as prince over the Kohathite family. Korach was burning with envy. And what did he do with that envy? Rashi tells us he dressed two hundred and fifty men in robes made entirely of techelet, the blue-purple wool used for the fringes of a garment, and brought them before Moses with a mocking question: Does a garment made entirely of purple still need a single thread of purple fringe? When Moses said yes, they laughed at him. A single thread exempts a garment, but a whole robe of the same color can't exempt itself? It was a clever rhetorical trap designed to make Moses look absurd.

<break time="0.75s" />

Rashi then draws our attention to Moses's reaction. The Torah says Moses fell on his face. Why didn't he simply pray, as he had done in earlier crises? Rashi explains that this was already the fourth time the people had sinned. After the Golden Calf, Moses prayed. After the complainers, Moses prayed. After the spies, Moses pleaded with God. But now, the fourth time, his hands dropped. Rashi offers a parable: it's like a prince who keeps sinning against his father, and a friend intercedes on his behalf — once, twice, three times. But the fourth time, the friend says, "How many more times can I impose on the king? Perhaps he won't accept my advocacy again." Moses felt he had exhausted his ability to intercede.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of Rashi's most poignant observations comes when Moses sends messengers to Dathan and Abiram. From this, Rashi draws a moral principle: one should not persist in a quarrel. Moses, the leader of the entire nation, personally reached out to these rebels to try to reconcile with peaceful words. He lowered himself for the sake of peace. And yet when Dathan and Abiram responded, "Lo na'aleh" — "We will not go up" — Rashi notes that their own mouths prophesied their fate. The words "we will not go up" unwittingly declared the truth: they would only go down, swallowed into the earth.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, after the rebellion is quashed and Aaron's staff miraculously blossoms, Rashi asks why the staff specifically produced almonds. His answer is elegant: the almond tree blossoms faster than any other fruit tree. This was a message. The punishment for anyone who challenges the divinely established priesthood comes swiftly. Rashi connects this to the later story of King Uzziah, who tried to offer incense in the Temple and was instantly struck with leprosy. The almond is a symbol of speed — of consequences that don't wait.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, we can see several powerful themes running through Rashi's commentary on this portion. First, there is his deep interest in human psychology — the envy that drives Korach, the exhaustion that overtakes Moses, the stubbornness of Dathan and Abiram. Rashi doesn't just explain words; he illuminates the inner lives of these characters. Second, Rashi repeatedly emphasizes that this rebellion was not merely against human leaders but against God's own design. The priestly roles, the tribal boundaries, even the separation of morning and evening — these are divine structures, and challenging them means challenging the Creator. Third, Rashi shows us the terrible cost of machloket, of communal strife and dissension. Even small children perished in this episode, something that wouldn't happen in any ordinary human court, because dissension tears apart the fabric of everything it touches. And through it all, Rashi reminds us that even within catastrophe, there is hope — Korach's own descendants repented, survived, and eventually became the great Temple singers whose psalms we still recite today.
