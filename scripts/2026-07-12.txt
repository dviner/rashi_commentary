Parashah (Rashi): Devarim (Deuteronomy 1:1-3:22)
Date: 2026-07-12

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rashi. This week we're looking at Parashat Devarim, the opening portion of the fifth and final book of the Torah, the Book of Deuteronomy. Let's dive in and see what Rashi reveals hiding beneath the surface of these verses.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parashat Devarim opens with Moses standing before the entire people of Israel on the plains of Moab, just weeks before his death and just before they will cross the Jordan River into the Promised Land without him. This is Moses's great farewell address — a sweeping look back at the forty years of wandering, the failures, the lessons, and the triumphs that brought them to this moment. He recounts the appointment of judges, the disastrous episode of the spies, the battles against Sihon and Og, and God's instructions about which nations to engage and which to leave in peace. What makes this portion especially rich for Rashi is that it is, at its core, a speech of reproof — Moses is rebuking the people for their past sins. And Rashi is fascinated by how Moses delivers that rebuke: with extraordinary tact, with careful timing, and with deep sensitivity to the dignity of the people he loves.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start with the very first verse. The Torah says, "These are the words that Moses spoke to all Israel, in the wilderness, in the Aravah, opposite Suph, between Paran and Tophel and Lavan." It reads like a list of geographical locations. But Rashi immediately asks: why does the Torah list all these places? His answer transforms the entire verse. These are not locations where Moses was standing — the people were on the plains of Moab, not in the wilderness. Rather, each place name is a coded reference to a sin the people had committed. "In the wilderness" alludes to their complaints about the manna. "Opposite Suph," meaning the Red Sea, recalls their panic when they said, "Were there no graves in Egypt?" "Tophel and Lavan" — Rashi notes that Rabbi Yochanan searched the entire Bible and found no place called Tophel or Lavan, so these must be wordplay: Tophel means slander, and Lavan means white, referring to how they slandered the white manna. "Di Zahav," meaning "enough gold," alludes to the golden calf. Moses is rebuking the people, but he does it through hints and allusions rather than spelling out their sins explicitly — out of respect for their dignity. This is not just a textual insight; it's a masterclass in how to give criticism with love.
<break time="0.75s" />
Next, Rashi addresses why Moses spoke "to all Israel." Why emphasize everyone? Because if Moses had rebuked only some of them, those who were absent could have said, "Had we been there, we would have answered him!" So Moses gathered the entire nation and said, in effect, "You are all here. If anyone has a response, speak now." Rashi shows us that Moses was not interested in embarrassing people or winning an argument. He wanted the rebuke to be fair, transparent, and unchallengeable.
<break time="0.75s" />
Now let's look at the timing. The Torah tells us this speech took place "in the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first of the month." Rashi calculates that Moses died on the seventh of the twelfth month — meaning he delivered this rebuke only about five weeks before his death. Why wait so long? Rashi says Moses learned this from the patriarch Jacob, who also rebuked his sons only on his deathbed. Jacob explained to his son Reuben: "I didn't rebuke you all these years because I was afraid you would leave me and join my brother Esau." Rashi then lists four reasons to wait until near death to give reproof — so you don't have to repeat it and cause recurring shame, so the person doesn't feel perpetually embarrassed in your presence. The timing of rebuke, Rashi teaches, is as important as its content.
<break time="0.75s" />
Another stunning comment comes on the verse about the eleven-day journey from Horeb to Kadesh Barnea. Rashi explains that Moses was saying: this journey should have taken only eleven days, and you actually did it in just three, because God's presence — the Shechinah — was rushing you toward the Promised Land. But because of the sin of the spies, that three-day sprint turned into forty years of wandering around Mount Seir. The contrast is devastating. God was so eager to bring you home that He compressed the journey, and you repaid that eagerness with faithlessness. Rashi transforms a geographical note into a profound meditation on squandered opportunity.
<break time="0.75s" />
Rashi also offers a remarkable comment on Moses's statement, "I cannot bear you alone." Was Moses really unable to judge the people? This is the man who split the sea and brought down the manna! Rashi says the real meaning is that God had placed the people above their judges — if a judge failed to prevent wrongdoing, the punishment fell on the judge, not the people. Moses was saying: the responsibility is unbearable, not because you are too numerous, but because the stakes for your judges are impossibly high. Even King Solomon echoed this when he asked God, "Who can judge this grievous people?" — not because he lacked wisdom, but because a Jewish judge who rules unjustly forfeits his very life.
<break time="0.75s" />
Finally, one of Rashi's most psychologically penetrating comments comes on the people's complaint, "Because the Lord hates us, He brought us out of Egypt." Rashi says: actually, God loved them, but they hated Him — and a common proverb teaches that what is in your own heart about your friend, you project onto your friend's heart about you. They assumed God hated them because they harbored resentment toward God. Rashi turns a moment of national complaint into a timeless observation about the psychology of projection — we see in others the feelings we carry within ourselves.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, what emerges from Rashi's commentary on this portion is a profound picture of what it means to lead, to teach, and to love a flawed people. Moses rebukes — but with coded language that preserves dignity. He waits until the end of his life so that shame won't linger. He gathers everyone so the rebuke is fair. He first defeats Sihon and Og, proving his credibility, before daring to criticize. Rashi's Moses is not just a prophet delivering God's word; he is a pastor, a psychologist, and a leader who understands that truth must be delivered with compassion or it will never be received. And through it all, Rashi reminds us that the way we read a text — noticing a strange place name, an unusual word, an odd repetition — is itself a spiritual act, a way of listening more carefully to what the Torah is really saying.
