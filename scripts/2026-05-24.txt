Parashah (Rashi): Nasso (Numbers 4:21-7:89)
Date: 2026-05-24

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, our weekly podcast where we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the master commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week's portion is called Nasso, from the book of Numbers, and it's actually the longest single Torah portion in the entire Torah. Let's dive in and see what Rashi finds hiding beneath the surface of these verses.

<break time="1.5s" />

Nasso covers an extraordinary range of topics. It begins with the completion of the Levite census and the assignment of duties for transporting the Tabernacle through the wilderness. Then it turns to laws of ritual purity in the camp, the law of someone who steals and swears falsely, the dramatic and mysterious ritual of the Sotah — the woman suspected of infidelity — and the laws of the Nazarite, the person who takes a special vow of holiness by abstaining from wine, haircuts, and contact with the dead. The portion concludes with the priestly blessing and the lengthy, repetitive account of the offerings brought by each tribal leader at the dedication of the Tabernacle. Rashi, characteristically, zeroes in on unusual word choices, logical gaps, and surprising juxtapositions between sections. He asks why certain laws are repeated, why one topic follows another, and what the precise language of each verse reveals about details we might otherwise miss.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's walk through some of Rashi's most striking comments on this portion. Our first stop is the laws of the three camps. The Torah commands that certain impure individuals be sent out of the camp, but Rashi explains that there wasn't just one camp — there were three concentric zones whenever Israel encamped in the wilderness. The innermost area, enclosed by the hangings of the courtyard, was the Camp of the Divine Presence. Surrounding that was the Camp of the Levites, and beyond that stretched the Camp of the Israelites. What's fascinating is that different types of impurity required different levels of distance. A leper was sent out of all three camps entirely. Someone with a bodily flux could remain in the outermost camp but was excluded from the two inner ones. And someone who had become impure through contact with a corpse was only excluded from the innermost camp. Rashi is showing us that impurity isn't a single, monolithic category — it's a spectrum, and the Torah's system reflects a carefully calibrated sense of spiritual geography.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at a passage that seems like a repetition. The Torah presents the case of someone who steals from another person and swears falsely about it. But Rashi points out that this law already appeared back in Leviticus. So why repeat it here in Numbers? Rashi answers that there are two new legal points embedded in this version. First, the Torah here uses the phrase "and they shall confess," which teaches that the penalty of paying an extra fifth and bringing a guilt offering applies only when the thief confesses voluntarily, not when convicted by witnesses. Second, this passage introduces the case of someone who stole from a convert who then died without heirs. Since a convert's non-Jewish relatives have no legal inheritance rights, the Torah needs to tell us what happens to the restitution — it goes to the priests. Rashi even raises a beautiful question from the rabbis: can there really be a person in Israel with no relative at all? Everyone traces back to Jacob! The answer is that only a convert who died could truly have no Jewish heir. This is a wonderful example of how Rashi uses an apparent redundancy in the Torah to uncover entirely new legal principles.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now we come to perhaps the most dramatic section: the Sotah, the suspected wife. The Torah describes an elaborate ritual involving bitter waters, a meal offering, and an oath. Rashi's comments here are layered and rich. He notes the unusual word the Torah uses for the wife's going astray — "tisteh" — and explains that the rabbis connected it to the word "shoteh," meaning someone who has gone mad. The teaching is striking: no one commits adultery unless a spirit of madness enters them. But Rashi doesn't stop there. He also gives the plain meaning of the text: the word simply means to deviate, to turn aside from the path of modesty, as in the verse from Proverbs, "turn away from it and pass by." What I love about this comment is how Rashi holds both readings together — the psychological insight that sin involves a kind of temporary insanity, and the straightforward reading that it's about stepping off the path. Both are true, and both illuminate different dimensions of human failure.

<break time="0.75s" />

Rashi also pays close attention to the physical details of the Sotah ritual, and in each one he finds symbolic meaning. The meal offering is made of barley flour, not fine wheat — because, Rashi explains, she acted in a bestial manner, so her offering consists of animal food. No oil is poured on it, because oil symbolizes light, and she acted in darkness. No frankincense is added, because frankincense symbolizes the matriarchs, and she deviated from their ways. Even the vessel matters: the water is drunk from a plain earthenware cup, because she served the adulterer fine wine in beautiful goblets. Every element of the ritual becomes a kind of moral mirror, reflecting back to the woman the nature of her transgression. Rashi transforms what could seem like an arcane procedure into a profound statement about measure-for-measure justice.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of Rashi's most famous observations in this portion involves why certain sections are placed next to each other. The laws of the Nazarite — the person who vows to abstain from wine, avoid contact with the dead, and let their hair grow — follow immediately after the Sotah passage. Why? Rashi answers simply and powerfully: whoever has witnessed a Sotah in her disgrace should abstain from wine, because wine can lead to adultery. The juxtaposition is not accidental; it's instructional. The Torah is telling us that witnessing the consequences of moral failure should inspire us to build protective fences around our own behavior. This is vintage Rashi — finding the connecting thread between seemingly unrelated passages and revealing the Torah's inner logic.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, a brief but striking comment on the Nazarite who accidentally becomes impure through contact with a dead body. The Torah says the priest shall make atonement for him "for that he sinned by the dead." But what sin did he commit if the contact was accidental? Rashi cites Rabbi Eleazar ha-Kappar, who offers a surprising answer: his sin is that he afflicted himself by abstaining from wine. The Torah itself calls the Nazarite holy, and yet here it also calls his vow a kind of sin. Rashi is comfortable holding this tension. Asceticism can be holy, but it can also be a form of self-denial that the Torah views with some ambivalence. This one small comment opens up an enormous conversation about the Jewish attitude toward pleasure, self-discipline, and the proper way to serve God.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, several themes emerge. Rashi throughout this portion is deeply attentive to the principle of measure for measure — the idea that consequences mirror actions, whether in the Sotah ritual or in the symbolic details of offerings. He's also fascinated by the architecture of the Torah itself: why laws are repeated, why sections sit next to each other, and what new meaning emerges from these structural choices. And perhaps most strikingly, Rashi shows us that the Torah holds complexity and even tension. The Nazarite is both holy and in need of atonement. The Sotah ritual is both a terrifying ordeal and a system designed, at every stage, to give the woman a chance to confess and avoid the erasure of God's name. Rashi reads the Torah as a text that trusts us with nuance.
