Parashah (Rashi): Lech-Lecha (Genesis 12:1-17:27)
Date: 2026-10-18

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, the podcast where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator whose insights have shaped Jewish learning for nearly a thousand years. This week we're looking at Parshat Lech-Lecha, which covers Genesis chapter twelve through chapter seventeen, and it's a portion absolutely brimming with drama, promise, and transformation. Let's dive in and see what Rashi notices that we might otherwise miss.

<break time="1.5s" />

Lech-Lecha is the portion where we meet Avram — later renamed Avraham — the first patriarch of the Jewish people. God tells him to leave his homeland and travel to a land that God will show him, promising to make him into a great nation. Along the way, Avram and his wife Sarai journey to Egypt during a famine, where Pharaoh takes Sarai into his palace, and God strikes Pharaoh with plagues to protect her. We also read about Avram's separation from his nephew Lot, a war between kings in which Avram rescues Lot, a dramatic covenant ceremony with God, the birth of Yishmael through Hagar, and finally God's command of circumcision and the renaming of both Avram and Sarai to Avraham and Sarah. Rashi, as always, is drawn to the small details — an unusual word, a grammatical oddity, a phrase that seems redundant — and he uses these tiny textual clues to unlock layers of meaning that enrich the entire narrative.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start with the very opening of the portion. God says to Avram, "Lech lecha," which literally means "Go for yourself," from your land, from your birthplace, and from your father's house. Now, most of us would describe a journey by starting small and going big — you'd say, leave your house, leave your city, leave your country. But the Torah does the opposite. It starts with "your land," the largest circle, and narrows down to "your father's house," the most intimate. Rashi picks up on this unusual order and explains that God structured the command this way to make each stage progressively more emotionally difficult. Leaving your country is hard, but leaving the specific place where you were born is harder, and leaving your father's household — the people you love most — is hardest of all. God is acknowledging Avram's emotional reality, and that small observation by Rashi transforms what could feel like a simple travel itinerary into a portrait of profound sacrifice.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at what happens when Avram and Sarai go down to Egypt. Because of the famine, they travel south, and Avram asks Sarai to say she is his sister, fearing the Egyptians will kill him to take her. Pharaoh does indeed take Sarai into his palace, but the Torah tells us, "And the Lord plagued Pharaoh." Rashi zeroes in on the nature of these plagues. He explains that Pharaoh was struck with a disease called raatan — a skin affliction that makes marital relations painful and harmful. This is a remarkably specific detail, and it matters because Rashi is telling us that God's intervention was precisely targeted. The plague wasn't random punishment; it was designed to protect Sarai in the most direct way possible, by making it physically impossible for Pharaoh to be intimate with her. Rashi reads God's justice as surgical and purposeful, matching the threat exactly.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's jump ahead to one of the most mysterious scenes in the portion — the Brit Bein HaBetarim, the Covenant Between the Parts, in chapter fifteen. God tells Avram that his descendants will be strangers in a land not their own, enslaved and oppressed for four hundred years. But when you count the actual years of slavery in Egypt as described later in the Torah, it doesn't add up to four hundred. Rashi addresses this apparent contradiction by explaining that the four hundred years are counted not from the beginning of the Egyptian slavery but from the birth of Yitzchak, Avraham's son, because from that moment the descendants of Avraham were living in a land that was "not theirs." Rashi is doing something characteristic here — he's harmonizing two parts of the Torah that seem to conflict, showing that the text is precise if you know how to read it carefully.

<break time="0.75s" />

Another beautiful Rashi comment comes when God changes Avram's name to Avraham in chapter seventeen. The Torah says God told him, "Your name shall no longer be called Avram, but your name shall be Avraham, for I have made you the father of a multitude of nations." Rashi explains the linguistics: Avram means "father of Aram," referring to his place of origin, but Avraham means "father of a multitude," av hamon. The letter hei is added to his name. Rashi notes that the letter reish in the original name, which pointed to Aram, is retained, because Avraham doesn't lose his past — he transcends it. His identity expands from being a local figure to a universal one, the father of many nations, and even the letters of his name carry that transformation.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, let's look at the circumcision itself at the end of the portion. The Torah describes both Avraham's circumcision and that of his son Yishmael, who was thirteen years old. In describing Yishmael's circumcision, the Torah uses the Hebrew word "et" — a grammatical particle that indicates a direct object — in the phrase "the flesh of his foreskin." But when describing Avraham's circumcision, this word "et" is absent. Rashi notices this tiny grammatical difference and offers a striking explanation. He says that Avraham, as an elderly man who had lived a full life, had a foreskin that was already naturally flattened, so circumcision required only the cutting of the skin. But Yishmael, being a young boy, required a more complete procedure — both cutting and the further step of uncovering. The extra word "et" signals this additional element. It's a perfect example of Rashi's method: a single small word, easily overlooked, becomes a window into the physical reality of the story and the precision of the Torah's language.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, what emerges from Rashi's commentary on Lech-Lecha is a deep conviction that every word in the Torah is chosen with care, and that no detail is accidental. Whether it's the order of three phrases in God's opening command, the nature of a plague in Egypt, or the presence or absence of a tiny grammatical particle, Rashi shows us that the Torah rewards close, patient reading. Across this portion, we also see Rashi painting a portrait of a God who is precise in justice, generous in promise, and deeply attentive to human experience — emotional, physical, and spiritual. And we see Avraham not as a flat heroic figure, but as a real person navigating sacrifice, fear, faith, and transformation. Rashi invites us to read the Torah not faster, but slower — and to trust that the deeper we look, the more we will find.
