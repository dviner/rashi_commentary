Parashah (Rashi): Eikev (Deuteronomy 7:12-11:25)
Date: 2026-07-26

Welcome back to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week's portion is called Eikev, found in the book of Deuteronomy, chapters seven through eleven. Let's dig into some of the fascinating insights Rashi offers on this rich and layered portion.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parshat Eikev continues Moses's great farewell address to the Israelites as they stand on the edge of the Promised Land. He urges them to keep God's commandments, promises them blessings if they do, and warns of consequences if they don't. He reminds them of the miracles in Egypt, the manna in the wilderness, the sin of the golden calf, and the breaking of the tablets. He describes the goodness of the land they're about to enter, contrasting it with Egypt, and he pleads with them to love God and serve Him wholeheartedly. Throughout this portion, Rashi is drawn to puzzles in the Hebrew language, to apparent contradictions between different biblical passages, and to moments where the Torah seems to say more or less than we'd expect. He's especially attentive to the emotional and moral dimensions of Moses's speech, reading between the lines to uncover layers of rebuke, encouragement, and theological depth.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start with the very first word of the portion, which actually gives it its name. The Torah says, "V'hayah eikev tishm'un," which is usually translated as something like, "And it shall come to pass, if you hearken to these commandments." But Rashi notices that the word eikev literally means "heel," as in the heel of your foot. So Rashi reads the verse this way: if you will heed even the lighter commandments, the ones a person tends to trample underfoot with their heel, the ones we're inclined to treat as unimportant, then God will keep His promise to you. Right from the opening word, Rashi is telling us that the Torah is concerned not just with the big, dramatic commandments, but with the small, everyday ones we might be tempted to dismiss. It's a powerful reminder that faithfulness is measured in the details.

<break time="0.75s" />

A little further on, in chapter eight, Moses tells the Israelites to keep "kol hamitzvah," which literally translates as "the whole commandment." Rashi offers two readings. On the plain level, it simply means every commandment, all of them. But then he shares a beautiful midrashic teaching: if you've begun a good deed, finish it, because a mitzvah, a commandment or meritorious act, is credited to the one who completes it. He brings the example of Joseph's bones. Moses was the one who retrieved them from Egypt, as it says in Exodus. But because Moses couldn't finish the task of burying them in the land of Israel, and the Israelites completed it, Scripture credits the burial to the Israelites. The lesson is striking: starting something good matters, but finishing it matters even more. Rashi draws this all from two little words, kol hamitzvah.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of the more vivid details in the portion comes when Moses recounts the forty years of wandering. He says, "Your garment did not wear out upon you, and your foot did not swell." Rashi brings a wonderful tradition to explain this. He says the clouds of Divine Glory, the miraculous clouds that accompanied Israel in the wilderness, would rub the dirt from their clothing and bleach them so they looked like new white garments. And even more remarkably, as the children grew, their clothes grew with them, the way the shell of a snail grows along with the snail inside it. As for their feet not swelling, Rashi connects the word "batzeka" to the word for dough, batzek. He's saying their feet didn't puff up like rising dough, as normally happens to people who walk barefoot for long distances. These little details transform the wilderness experience from a story of hardship into a story of ongoing, intimate divine care.

<break time="0.75s" />

When Moses describes the Land of Israel, he says it is "not like the land of Egypt." Now, you might read that as a compliment or as a criticism. Rashi raises exactly this question. Is Moses saying the land is better than Egypt, or worse? To answer, Rashi launches into a fascinating argument involving the cities of Hebron and Zoan. The Torah tells us elsewhere that Hebron was built seven years before Zoan. Rashi explains that Ham, the son of Noah, built both cities, Zoan for his son Mizraim, who is Egypt, and Hebron for his son Canaan. Naturally, a father builds the finer city for his firstborn. So Hebron, the worst part of Canaan, was still superior to Zoan, which was the finest part of Egypt, being the seat of royalty. And Egypt itself was already compared to the Garden of God. The conclusion? The Land of Israel far surpasses even the greatest land the Israelites had ever known. Beyond this, Rashi contrasts the agricultural reality of the two lands. Egypt required backbreaking labor, hauling water from the Nile by foot, irrigating by hand, with only the lowlands receiving water. But the Land of Israel drinks the rain of heaven. You can sleep soundly in your bed, Rashi says, and God waters both the hills and the valleys for you.

<break time="0.75s" />

In chapter eleven, the Torah uses a doubled verb form: "shamoa tishm'u," which literally means something like "hearkening, you shall hearken." Rashi reads this doubling as conveying a profound educational principle. If you listen to the old, if you review and revisit what you've already learned, then you will be able to hear the new, to absorb fresh knowledge. And conversely, the Torah later uses a similar construction about forgetting: "if you begin to forget, you will end up forgetting everything." Rashi even cites a teaching written in a certain scroll: if you forget Me for one day, I will forget you for two days. Learning, in Rashi's view, is not a one-time event. It requires constant return and renewal. And connected to this, Rashi comments on the phrase "which I command you this day," saying the commandments should always feel as fresh and new as if you heard them for the very first time today. Repetition and freshness are not opposites; they sustain each other.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, near the end of the portion, the Torah commands us "to walk in all His ways." What does it mean to walk in God's ways? Rashi's answer is elegant and direct: He is merciful, so you be merciful. He bestows lovingkindness, so you bestow lovingkindness. And then the Torah says to "cleave to Him." Rashi asks the obvious question: how can a person cleave to God, who is described elsewhere as a consuming fire? His answer: attach yourself to Torah scholars and sages, and God will consider it as though you have cleaved to Him. In just a few words, Rashi transforms an abstract theological idea into a concrete, human practice. The way to draw close to the infinite is through community, through learning alongside others, through the relationships we build around shared pursuit of wisdom.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, we can see several threads running through Rashi's reading of Parshat Eikev. One is the theme of divine care in the details, whether it's the clothes that don't wear out, the rain that falls at night so as not to disturb you, or the commandments that seem small but matter immensely. Another is the theme of memory and renewal: the constant need to revisit, review, and re-experience the Torah as though hearing it for the first time. There's also a recurring tension between complacency and gratitude. Rashi warns, drawing on the Torah's own language, that fullness and prosperity are precisely the moments when people are most likely to forget God. And finally, there's Rashi's characteristic move of grounding lofty theology in human action. Walk in God's ways by being merciful. Cleave to God by attaching yourself to wise teachers. Throughout it all, Rashi shows us that the Torah speaks not just to a generation standing at the border of Canaan, but to every reader who struggles with the daily challenge of paying attention, staying faithful, and finding holiness in the ordinary.
