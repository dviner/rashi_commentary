Parashah (Rashi): Noach (Genesis 6:9-11:32)
Date: 2026-10-11

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, your weekly podcast where we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator Rashi, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki. This week we're looking at Parshat Noach, which covers Genesis chapter six verse nine through the end of chapter eleven, and it's a portion packed with some of the most dramatic stories in the entire Torah. Let's dive in and see what Rashi notices that we might otherwise miss.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parshat Noach tells the story of a world gone wrong and God's decision to start over. It begins with Noah, described as a righteous man in his generation, who is commanded to build an ark to survive a catastrophic flood that will destroy all life on earth. After the floodwaters recede, Noah emerges, offers sacrifices, and God establishes a covenant with humanity, symbolized by the rainbow. But the portion doesn't end on that hopeful note. Noah plants a vineyard, becomes drunk, and a disturbing incident occurs involving his son Ham. The portion then moves on to the Table of Nations, the descendants of Noah's three sons, and culminates with the story of the Tower of Babel, where humanity's attempt to build a tower to the heavens leads God to scatter them across the earth and confuse their languages. Rashi, throughout this portion, is drawn to moments of moral ambiguity, to words that seem unnecessary or oddly chosen, and to gaps in the narrative where the Torah seems to leave something unsaid, inviting us to look more closely.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start with the very first verse of the portion. The Torah says, "These are the generations of Noah. Noah was a righteous man, perfect in his generations." Rashi immediately picks up on something curious: the phrase "in his generations." Why does the Torah qualify Noah's righteousness this way? Why not simply say he was righteous? Rashi presents two interpretations from the Talmud. Some rabbis read it favorably: if Noah could be righteous even in a corrupt generation, imagine how much greater he would have been in a righteous generation. Others read it less generously: Noah was only righteous relative to his wicked contemporaries, but had he lived in Abraham's time, he wouldn't have been considered special at all. What's beautiful here is that Rashi doesn't choose between the two readings. He lets the tension stand, teaching us that the Torah's ambiguity is itself meaningful. It invites us to think about what it really means to be a good person — is goodness absolute, or is it always shaped by context?

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at a striking comment Rashi makes about why God chose a flood specifically, and why He told Noah to build an ark rather than simply saving him some other way. The Torah describes God's instructions to build the ark in great detail, and Rashi asks the obvious question: God could have saved Noah in any number of ways, so why make him spend decades constructing this enormous vessel? Rashi explains that the lengthy building process was meant to provoke questions. People would see Noah building and ask what he was doing. He would tell them that God was about to bring a flood because of their wickedness, and perhaps — just perhaps — they would repent. The ark, in other words, was not just a lifeboat. It was an invitation. It was God giving humanity every possible chance to change course. This tells us something profound about divine justice in Rashi's understanding: punishment is never God's first choice. Even when destruction is decreed, the door to repentance remains open until the very last moment.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to a moment after the flood that reveals something unexpected about Noah himself. The Torah says, in chapter nine, "And Noah began to be a man of the soil, and he planted a vineyard." The Hebrew word the Torah uses is "vayachel," which can mean "he began," but Rashi points out that this word is related to the Hebrew word for "profane" or "mundane." Rashi says Noah made himself profane, because the first thing he should have planted was something more useful or dignified — not a vineyard. He should have occupied himself with a different planting first. This is a subtle but devastating critique. Here is the man who survived the destruction of the entire world, who walked with God, who was given the chance to rebuild civilization — and his first act is to plant grapes so he can make wine. Rashi is drawing our attention to a kind of spiritual anticlimax. Noah was righteous enough to be saved, but the Torah doesn't let us turn him into a flawless hero. Even the best people stumble, and Rashi wants us to see that honestly.

<break time="0.75s" />

The story gets darker from there. Noah becomes drunk and lies uncovered in his tent. The Torah tells us that Ham, the father of Canaan, "saw his father's nakedness" and told his two brothers outside. Rashi, drawing on the Talmud in Tractate Sanhedrin, says that the phrase "saw his father's nakedness" implies more than mere looking. The sages suggest that Ham either castrated his father or violated him. This is a deeply uncomfortable comment, and Rashi includes it precisely because the text seems to demand it. After all, when Noah wakes up, the Torah says he "knew what his youngest son had done to him" — the word "done" implies an action, not merely an observation. And Noah's response is to curse not Ham but Ham's son Canaan, which seems disproportionate if all Ham did was accidentally see something. Rashi is teaching us to read carefully, to notice when the Torah's language hints at something more severe beneath the surface. The punishment must fit the crime, and if the punishment is this harsh, the crime must have been grievous.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, let's look at the Tower of Babel story near the end of the portion. The Torah says the people said, "Come, let us build a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, and let us make for ourselves a name, lest we be scattered across the face of the earth." Rashi asks what their sin actually was. After all, building a city doesn't sound inherently evil. Rashi explains that they were rebelling against God — the phrase "let us make for ourselves a name" suggests they wanted to wage war against the divine, to assert their own power and autonomy in defiance of their Creator. The irony, which Rashi helps us see, is that the very thing they feared — being scattered — is exactly what happens to them as a consequence of their arrogance. They tried to prevent their dispersion through a grand unified project, and their rebellion brought about the very outcome they dreaded. It's a powerful lesson about how human attempts to defy the natural moral order tend to be self-defeating.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, we can see several themes running through Rashi's commentary on this portion. One is the complexity of righteousness. Noah is righteous but flawed, praised but qualified, saved but not spared from his own weaknesses. Rashi refuses to flatten biblical characters into simple heroes or villains. Another theme is divine patience and justice — God gives humanity chance after chance, whether through the decades-long construction of the ark or through the covenant of the rainbow. And a third theme is the importance of precise reading. Again and again, Rashi shows us that a single unusual word — "in his generations," "vayachel," "saw," "done" — can unlock an entire layer of meaning that a casual reader would miss. For Rashi, every word in the Torah is deliberate, and our job as readers is to honor that deliberateness with our attention.
