Parashah (Rashi): Beha'alotcha (Numbers 8:1-12:16)
Date: 2026-05-31

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known simply as Rashi. This week we're looking at Parshat Beha'alotcha, which covers Numbers chapters eight through twelve, and as always, Rashi has so much to show us about what's really going on beneath the surface of the text.

<break time="1.5s" />

Beha'alotcha is one of those Torah portions that covers a lot of ground. It opens with instructions about lighting the menorah, the golden candelabrum in the Tabernacle, and then moves to the consecration of the Levites for their special service. We get laws about the Passover offering, including a fascinating case where certain people who missed Passover petition Moses for a second chance. Then the Torah describes the cloud that rested over the Tabernacle, the silver trumpets used to signal the camp, and the departure of the Israelites from Mount Sinai. But the portion takes a sharp turn in its final chapters: the people begin complaining, first about the hardships of travel and then about the food, longing for the fish they ate in Egypt. Rashi, true to form, zeroes in on unusual word choices, apparent contradictions, and gaps in logic throughout this portion. He asks why sections are placed where they are, why specific words are used instead of simpler ones, and what the characters' inner lives look like when the text only gives us the barest sketch.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's walk through some of Rashi's most striking comments on this portion, starting right at the very beginning. The Torah opens by telling Aaron to light the lamps of the menorah. Rashi immediately asks a structural question: why does the section about the menorah come right after the lengthy account of the tribal princes bringing their dedication offerings? His answer is deeply human. When Aaron saw all twelve princes offering their elaborate gifts for the Tabernacle's dedication, he felt distressed because neither he nor his tribe, the Levites, had been included. God reassured him by saying, your role is even greater than theirs, because you will kindle and arrange the lamps. Rashi draws this from the Midrash Tanchuma, and it's a beautiful example of how he reads the Torah not just as a legal document but as a story about real people with real emotions, people who can feel left out and who need reassurance even from God.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's look at a fascinating textual detail in that same opening verse. The Torah doesn't use the ordinary Hebrew word for lighting the lamps. Instead it says beha'alotcha, which literally means when you cause them to rise or go up. Rashi picks up on this unusual word and draws two lessons from it. First, practically, the priest must hold the flame to the wick until it rises on its own, until the lamp burns independently. Second, the Rabbis derived from this language that there must have been a step in front of the menorah for the priest to stand on while tending the flames. This is classic Rashi: one unusual word, and he extracts both a vivid practical image and a legal principle.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving further into the portion, we encounter the story of certain men who were ritually impure because of contact with a dead body and therefore could not bring the Passover offering at the proper time. They come before Moses and Aaron and ask a remarkable question: lamah nigara, why should we be kept back? Rashi fills in the dialogue. Moses had told them that people in a state of impurity cannot offer sacrifices. They pushed back, arguing creatively: let ritually clean priests sprinkle the blood on our behalf and let the clean eat the meat. Moses, rather than answering immediately, says, stand still and I will hear what God commands concerning you. Rashi pauses to marvel at this moment. He describes Moses as a student who is confident he can consult his teacher at any time, and then adds, happy indeed is a person born of woman who can so confidently rely that whenever he wishes, he may speak with the Shechinah, with God's presence. Rashi also notes something remarkable about this passage: this law of the Second Passover should by rights have been taught by Moses directly, like the rest of the Torah. But these men merited that it was promulgated through their initiative, because meritorious deeds are brought about through worthy people.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of Rashi's most structurally important comments comes in the middle of the portion, at the famous two verses beginning with "Rise up, O Lord" and "Return, O Lord," which in Torah scrolls are set off by inverted Hebrew letters shaped like the letter nun. Rashi explains that these marks indicate that this passage is not in its proper place. It should logically appear elsewhere, in the section describing the marching order of the camps. So why is it here? To create a break between one narrative of punishment and another. The Torah is about to describe the people complaining, and shortly before that there was another failing. By inserting this passage between them, the Torah avoids placing two accounts of Israel's failures directly next to each other. This is a striking insight into how the Torah is edited and arranged, and it shows Rashi's awareness that the Torah's structure carries meaning just as much as its words do.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, let's look at the bitter complaints about food near the end of the portion. The mixed multitude and then the Israelites themselves begin weeping and crying out, who will give us meat to eat? We remember the fish we ate in Egypt for free! Rashi asks a sharp question: did they not have meat? The Torah itself tells us they left Egypt with flocks and herds in great abundance, and later, when they're about to enter the land, the tribes of Reuben and Gad still have enormous herds. So clearly they had meat. The truth, Rashi says, is that they were simply seeking a pretext to complain. And what about eating fish for free in Egypt? Rashi asks another pointed question: the Egyptians wouldn't even give them straw for free, so would they have given them fish for nothing? The word chinam, for free, Rashi explains, doesn't mean free of monetary cost. It means free of commandments. What the people were really longing for was not the cuisine of Egypt but the life without religious obligation. They missed a world where they could eat without thinking about what was permitted and what was forbidden. This is one of Rashi's most psychologically penetrating readings: beneath the surface complaint about food lies a deeper rebellion against the weight of spiritual responsibility.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, several themes emerge from Rashi's treatment of Beha'alotcha. One is his deep attention to the emotional lives of the Torah's characters: Aaron's distress, Moses' confidence before God, the people's restlessness. Another is his insistence that the Torah's arrangement is deliberate and meaningful, that even when passages seem out of order, there is a reason for their placement. A third theme, perhaps the most sobering, is Rashi's unflinching honesty about human nature. The complaints about meat weren't really about meat. The longing for Egypt wasn't really about fish. People sometimes dress up their deeper resistances in the language of practical grievances. Rashi teaches us to read the Torah, and perhaps to read ourselves, with that same honesty, looking past the surface to the real question underneath.
