Parashah (Rashi): Rosh Hashana I (Genesis 21:1-34)
Date: 2026-09-06

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week we're looking at the special reading for Rosh Hashana, the Jewish New Year, which is taken from Genesis chapter twenty-one, and it is packed with dramatic moments that Rashi brings to life with his signature close reading.

<break time="1.5s" />

This portion tells the story of one of the most anticipated events in the Torah: the birth of Isaac to Abraham and Sarah, after decades of waiting. Sarah, who had been barren her entire life, finally gives birth in her old age, just as God had promised. Abraham makes a great feast when the child is weaned. But joy quickly gives way to tension. Sarah sees Ishmael, the son Abraham had with Hagar, behaving in a way she finds threatening, and she demands that Abraham send Hagar and Ishmael away. Abraham is deeply distressed, but God tells him to listen to Sarah. Hagar and Ishmael wander in the wilderness, nearly dying of thirst before God opens their eyes to a well. The portion closes with Abraham making a treaty with Abimelech at Be'er Sheva. Rashi, as always, zeroes in on unusual words, structural puzzles, and moral complexities that we might otherwise glide right past.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start at the very beginning of the chapter. The Torah says, "And the Lord visited Sarah," and this comes right after the story of Abraham praying for Abimelech's household to be healed of infertility. Rashi asks a structural question: why are these two stories placed side by side? His answer is a powerful spiritual principle. When a person prays on behalf of another for something that he himself also needs, the one who prays is answered first. Abraham prayed for Abimelech's wife to bear children, and Abraham himself needed that same blessing for Sarah. So God remembered Sarah even before He healed Abimelech. The placement of these passages isn't accidental; it's the Torah's way of teaching us that generosity in prayer is rewarded.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at one of Rashi's most striking comments. When Isaac is weaned and Abraham throws a feast, the Torah describes it as a "mishteh gadol," a great feast. What makes it great? Rashi says it's called great because the great people of the generation were present: Shem, Eber, and Abimelech. The word "great" doesn't describe the quantity of food or the lavishness of the celebration. It describes the stature of the guests. This is classic Rashi, taking a seemingly simple adjective and showing us that it carries specific, meaningful information about the event.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now we come to the most dramatic and troubling moment in the portion. Sarah sees Ishmael "metzachek," a word usually translated as "laughing" or "playing." But Rashi insists this is no innocent play. He brings three interpretations from the Midrash: the word can refer to idol worship, as it does when describing the Golden Calf; it can refer to sexual immorality, as it does in the story of Potiphar's wife; or it can refer to murder, as it does in the story of Abner's men who "played" before killing each other. Rashi then adds that Ishmael was quarreling with Isaac over the inheritance, claiming the double portion of a firstborn, and that he took Isaac out into the field and shot arrows at him, pretending it was just a game. This is why Sarah reacts so fiercely. She isn't being petty or jealous. She sees a genuine threat to her son's life and spiritual future. And when she says, "The son of this bondwoman shall not inherit with my son, with Isaac," Rashi unpacks her precise language: even if he weren't Isaac, as my son he would deserve the inheritance over Ishmael; and even if he weren't my son, as the righteous man Isaac is, he would still deserve it. How much more so when both qualities are combined. Sarah's words are not redundant. They are a carefully constructed legal and moral argument.

<break time="0.75s" />

When Abraham is distressed about sending Ishmael away, God tells him, "Shema b'kolah," listen to her voice. Rashi draws a remarkable conclusion from this phrase: Abraham was inferior to Sarah in prophecy. God is essentially telling Abraham that Sarah's spiritual perception in this matter is more accurate than his own. This is a striking comment, elevating Sarah's prophetic authority and reminding us that moral clarity sometimes comes from unexpected directions.

<break time="0.75s" />

Perhaps the most theologically profound comment Rashi makes in this portion concerns the moment when Ishmael is dying of thirst in the wilderness. The Torah says God heard "the voice of the lad where he is." Rashi explains that the phrase "where he is" — "ba'asher hu sham" — means God judges a person according to their present actions, not according to what they may do in the future. The angels protested, arguing that Ishmael's descendants would one day kill Israelites with thirst. But God asked a single question: right now, is he righteous or wicked? The angels answered: righteous. And God said, then I will judge him as he is right now. Rashi goes on to tell the devastating story of how Ishmael's descendants would later offer inflated, empty waterskins to thirsty Israelite exiles, who died when they opened them and swallowed only air. Yet despite this terrible future, God saved the boy in the present moment. This comment teaches something profound about divine justice: mercy is extended based on who a person is in this moment, not who they might become. For Rosh Hashana, a day of judgment, this could not be more relevant.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, at the end of the portion, Abraham plants an "eshel" at Be'er Sheva. Rashi brings a disagreement between two great sages, Rav and Shmuel: one says it was an orchard to provide fruit for guests, and the other says it was an inn stocked with all kinds of provisions. But either way, the purpose was hospitality. And Rashi adds that through this eshel, Abraham would call out the name of God to all who came. After his guests ate and drank, he would tell them: don't thank me, thank the One whose food you have actually eaten, the One who spoke and the universe came into being. Abraham's hospitality wasn't just kindness. It was a vehicle for spreading awareness of God throughout the world.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, we can see several themes running through Rashi's commentary on this portion. There is the theme of divine promise and fulfillment: God said Sarah would bear a son, and every detail of that promise was kept, down to the exact timing. There is the theme of justice and judgment: who deserves the inheritance, how God evaluates a person in the present moment, and what constitutes genuine threat versus apparent play. And there is the theme of spiritual perception: Sarah's prophetic insight, Abraham's generous prayer, and the way hospitality becomes a form of teaching about God. Rashi shows us that every word in the Torah is doing work, that nothing is accidental, and that beneath the surface of these ancient stories lie principles that speak directly to how we understand mercy, justice, and faithfulness, themes that are at the very heart of Rosh Hashana.
