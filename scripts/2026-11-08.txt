Parashah (Rashi): Toldot (Genesis 25:19-28:9)
Date: 2026-11-08

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, the podcast where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator whose insights have shaped how Jews read the Bible for nearly a thousand years. This week we're looking at Parshat Toldot, which means "generations" or "offspring," covering Genesis chapter 25 verse 19 through chapter 28 verse 9. Let's dive in and see what Rashi finds hiding beneath the surface of this rich and dramatic portion.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parshat Toldot is one of the great family dramas of the Torah. It begins with Isaac and Rebecca struggling with infertility, then tells of the turbulent pregnancy and birth of their twin sons, Jacob and Esau. As the boys grow, they become strikingly different — Esau the hunter, Jacob the quiet tent-dweller. The portion then moves through Isaac's sojourn among the Philistines, where he passes Rebecca off as his sister, and his disputes over wells. It culminates in the famous scene where Jacob, guided by Rebecca, disguises himself as Esau to receive his father's blessing. Rashi, as always, is drawn to the small details that don't quite add up — an unusual word, a seemingly unnecessary phrase, a moment where the text raises more questions than it answers. In this portion, he's especially attentive to the question of character: who are Jacob and Esau really, and how does the Torah signal the truth about each of them even in the smallest turns of phrase?

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start at the very beginning of the portion, with a detail that seems almost redundant. The Torah says, "Abraham begot Isaac," right after telling us that Isaac is the son of Abraham. Why the repetition? Rashi explains that the scoffers of the generation were saying that Sarah had actually conceived from Avimelekh, the Philistine king, since she had lived in his household and had never become pregnant from Abraham. So what did God do? God shaped Isaac's facial features to look exactly like Abraham's, so that everyone could see the resemblance and no one could deny the paternity. This is classic Rashi — he notices the redundancy in the text and uses it to uncover a social reality behind the story, showing us that Isaac's legitimacy mattered and that the Torah goes out of its way to affirm it.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at the birth of the twins. The Torah tells us that when Esau came out, Jacob was grasping his brother's heel. It's a vivid image, but what does it mean? Rashi shares a midrash — a rabbinic teaching — that explains it in a surprisingly logical way. He compares the womb to a narrow tube. If you drop two stones into a tube one after another, the one that goes in first comes out last, and the one that goes in last comes out first. Rashi says Jacob was actually conceived first but, because of the mechanics of the womb, Esau emerged first. So Jacob was grabbing Esau's heel with justice — he was trying to reclaim his rightful position as the true firstborn, the one who was conceived first. This reframes the entire story of the birthright. It's not that Jacob was a schemer stealing something that didn't belong to him. According to Rashi, Jacob had a legitimate claim from the very moment of conception.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's move to the scene where Isaac is living among the Philistines and has told them that Rebecca is his sister, just as his father Abraham once did with Sarah. The Torah says that Avimelekh, the king, looked out his window and saw Isaac "sporting" or "playing" with Rebecca. The Hebrew word used here is "metzachek." Rashi cuts right to the point: Avimelekh saw Isaac being intimate with Rebecca, engaging in marital relations. The word "metzachek" tipped Avimelekh off that these two were clearly not brother and sister. Rashi then notes Avimelekh's angry rebuke — "You would have brought guilt upon us" — and explains that this means if someone had already slept with Rebecca, the guilt would have fallen on the entire community. The stakes here are moral and communal, not just personal, and Rashi makes sure we see how seriously the text treats even a potential violation.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, let's look at one of the most emotionally complex moments in the portion. When Isaac asks Esau to hunt game and prepare a meal so he can bless him, Rebecca overhears and orchestrates Jacob's disguise. When Jacob approaches his father wearing Esau's clothes and goatskins on his arms, Isaac says the famous words, "The voice is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau." Rashi draws our attention to how Isaac was nearly deceived but sensed something was off. The voice — the quality of speech, the way Jacob spoke with courtesy and mentioned God's name — that was unmistakably Jacob. But the hands, rough and hairy, those seemed to belong to Esau. Rashi shows us that even in this moment of deception, the Torah preserves a fundamental truth about each brother's nature. Jacob's essence comes through in his voice, in his spiritual quality, while Esau's identity is associated with physicality, with the rough hands of a hunter. The disguise could cover the outside but couldn't fully mask what lay within.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, what emerges from Rashi's commentary on Toldot is a sustained meditation on identity and legitimacy. Again and again, Rashi asks: who truly deserves what? Isaac's face must prove he is Abraham's son. Jacob's grasp on Esau's heel asserts a claim that goes back to the moment of conception. Even the scene with Avimelekh raises questions about how identity — sister or wife — carries real consequences. Rashi reads the Torah as a text that cares deeply about truth beneath appearances. His method reminds us that the Torah's meaning often lives in its smallest details — a repeated phrase, an unusual word, a gesture that seems minor but carries enormous weight. For Rashi, close reading is itself a form of reverence, a way of honoring the idea that every word of Torah is there for a reason.
