Parashah (Rashi): Matot-Masei (Numbers 30:2-36:13)
Date: 2026-07-05

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, the podcast where we explore the weekly Torah portion through the sharp and beautiful lens of Rashi, the great medieval commentator. This week we're looking at the double portion of Matot-Masei, which takes us from Numbers chapter 30 verse 2 all the way through the end of the book of Numbers, chapter 36 verse 13. Let's see what Rashi has to teach us.

<break time="1.5s" />

Matot-Masei is a sweeping double portion that covers an enormous amount of ground. It begins with the laws of vows and oaths, moves into the war against the Midianites and the division of its spoils, continues with the request of the tribes of Reuben and Gad to settle on the eastern side of the Jordan River, and then in the second portion lists all forty-two stops the Israelites made during their forty years of wandering. It also includes instructions for conquering and dividing the Land of Israel. The portion covers law, warfare, negotiation, and memory, essentially wrapping up the entire wilderness narrative. Rashi, as always, zeroes in on textual oddities: a word that seems unnecessary, a phrase that could be misread, a sequence that hints at a deeper legal or moral principle. In this portion, he's especially attentive to questions of legal precision in the laws of vows, to the moral character of the Israelite soldiers, and to the emotional relationships between leaders and their people.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start at the very beginning. The Torah opens this portion by saying that Moses spoke to the heads of the tribes about the laws of vows. Rashi immediately asks: does this mean Moses spoke only to the tribal leaders and not to the rest of the people? That would be strange, since all of the Torah's laws are meant for everyone. Rashi explains that the standard procedure was always to teach the leaders first and then the people, as we know from Exodus, where Aaron and the princes heard Moses first and then the rest of Israel came near. So why does the Torah go out of its way to mention the tribal heads here? Because this specific mention teaches something about the annulment of vows: ideally, a vow should be annulled by a single legal expert, and only if no expert is available may a panel of three ordinary people do so. That detail is encoded in the very way the verse is addressed, a classic example of Rashi finding legal meaning hidden in what seems like a routine introduction.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at the phrase "zeh hadavar," meaning "this is the word," which introduces the laws of vows. Rashi notices that other prophets introduced their prophecies with the phrase "ko amar Hashem," meaning "thus says the Lord," but Moses uniquely also used the phrase "this is the word." The difference is subtle but important: "thus says the Lord" is an approximation, a paraphrase, while "this is the word" implies an exact, precise transmission. Moses had a clarity of prophecy that went beyond all other prophets. He didn't just convey God's message; he conveyed it with word-for-word precision. This seemingly small textual observation becomes, in Rashi's hands, a statement about Moses's unique spiritual stature.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving into the war against Midian, the Torah tells us that God instructed Moses to take vengeance on the Midianites. Rashi asks a question that might occur to any careful reader: why Midian and not Moab? After all, the Moabites were also involved in the plot to corrupt Israel. Rashi gives two answers. First, a practical one: the Moabites acted out of fear, since Israel was camped on their doorstep, whereas the Midianites inserted themselves into a conflict that had nothing to do with them. Second, a more poetic reason: God was protecting the future. Two righteous women were destined to come from Moab and Ammon: Ruth the Moabitess, who would become the great-grandmother of King David, and Naamah the Ammonitess, who would become the wife of King Solomon. God's long view of history shaped the military orders of the present. This is Rashi at his most breathtaking, connecting a battlefield decision to a genealogical thread that stretches across centuries.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now here's one of my favorite Rashi comments in this portion. The Torah says that Moses sent the troops to war, and they were "handed over," using the Hebrew word "vayimasru." Rashi reads enormous emotional weight into that word. He says it tells us how beloved Moses was to the people. Earlier in the wilderness, the people had been ready to stone him. But now, when they learned that Moses's death was linked to the completion of this war against Midian, they refused to go. They had to be handed over against their will, because they didn't want the war to end if it meant losing Moses. The same people who once threatened him with stones now clung to him with love. Rashi finds in a single verb a story of transformation, of a relationship between a leader and his people that deepened over forty hard years.

<break time="0.75s" />

After the war, the soldiers bring back the spoils, and Moses becomes angry with the officers for keeping certain Midianite women alive. Rashi uses this moment of anger to teach something remarkable about Moses's humanity. He says that when Moses fell into anger, he fell into error: his rage caused him to forget the laws about purging vessels that had absorbed forbidden food from the Midianites. It was Eleazar the priest, not Moses, who had to teach those laws. And Rashi notes this wasn't the only time: the same pattern of anger leading to error occurred when Moses struck the rock instead of speaking to it, and again on the eighth day of the Tabernacle's inauguration. Even the greatest prophet was not immune to the distorting effects of anger. Rashi draws this pattern across multiple episodes, making a quiet but powerful moral point.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, at the opening of Masei, the Torah lists all forty-two places where the Israelites camped during their forty years in the wilderness. Rashi asks the obvious question: why do we need this long, seemingly tedious travel log? He offers a beautiful parable from Rabbi Tanchuma. Imagine a king whose son was ill, and the king took him on a long journey to find a cure. On the way home, the father pointed out every stop along the way: here we rested, here you had a headache, here we caught cold. The listing of the stations is not bureaucratic record-keeping. It's an act of love. God is looking back with His people at everything they went through together, every stop on the long, difficult road. Rashi also adds a mathematical observation: if you subtract the stops from the first year before the decree of wandering and the stops from the final year after Aaron's death, the Israelites only made twenty journeys over thirty-eight years. Their wandering wasn't the ceaseless, punishing march we might imagine. Even in exile, there was rest.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, several themes emerge from Rashi's commentary across this double portion. One is the theme of precision: in vows, in prophecy, in legal categories, the exact word matters. "This is the word" is not the same as "thus says the Lord," and the difference between annulling and invalidating a vow is not mere semantics but determines whether the vow still stands. Another theme is the humanity of leaders. Moses delays nothing even when he knows the war will hasten his death, yet his anger causes him to forget the law. The officers are held responsible for the misdeeds of their generation. Leadership in Rashi's reading is both noble and burdensome. And perhaps most beautifully, there is the theme of divine love expressed through memory. The listing of the stations isn't a footnote to the wilderness story; it is its emotional culmination, a father and child looking back together at the long road home.
