Parashah (Rashi): Bereshit (Genesis 1:1-6:8)
Date: 2026-10-04

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, the podcast where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator whose brilliant, concise readings have shaped how Jews have understood the Bible for nearly a thousand years. This week we're looking at Parashat Bereshit, the very first portion in the Torah, Genesis chapter one through chapter six verse eight, and there is no better place to begin than at the beginning itself — with Rashi as our guide.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parashat Bereshit covers an enormous sweep of narrative. It opens with the creation of the world in six days and God's rest on the seventh. It then zooms in on the creation of Adam and Eve, their life in the Garden of Eden, the temptation of the serpent, and their expulsion from paradise. From there, we read about Cain and Abel — the first siblings and the first murder — and then the Torah races through ten generations of humanity, ending with the birth of Noah and a world so corrupt that God regrets having created it. These are foundational stories, dense with meaning, and Rashi finds questions everywhere. He notices unusual word choices in the very first verse. He asks why the Torah begins where it does. He probes the psychology of the characters — what motivated Eve, what was Cain really feeling, how did God speak to them and why in that particular way. In a portion this rich, Rashi's comments range from the grammatical to the theological, and today we'll walk through several of his most striking observations.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start right at the very first verse of the Torah: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." You might think this is a simple, declarative statement, but Rashi immediately raises a profound question — why does the Torah begin with the story of creation at all? The Torah, after all, is primarily a book of laws, of commandments given to the Jewish people. Wouldn't it have made more sense to begin with the first commandment given to Israel as a nation? Rashi, citing Rabbi Yitzchak, explains that the Torah opens with creation to establish that God created the entire world and therefore has the authority to give any land to any nation He chooses. If the nations of the world ever say to Israel, "You are thieves, you conquered the land from other peoples," Israel can reply, "The whole earth belongs to God. He created it and gave it to whomever He saw fit." This is a remarkable opening move by Rashi. Before we even get into the details of the creation narrative, he's asking us to think about the purpose of the text itself — why it's structured the way it is, and what theological claim it's making about God's sovereignty over the world.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at the creation of humanity. The Torah says in chapter one, verse twenty-six, "Let us make man in our image." That plural — "let us" — is startling. If God is one, who is He speaking to? Rashi acknowledges that this language could be misused to suggest that multiple divine beings were involved in creation. But he explains that God was speaking to the angels, consulting with them out of humility, to teach proper conduct — that a greater being should consult with and seek the agreement of lesser ones before acting. Rashi adds that even though this phrasing risks misunderstanding, the Torah uses it anyway because the ethical lesson about humility is too important to omit. And he points out that the very next verse switches to the singular — "And God created man" — making clear that God alone did the creating. This is classic Rashi: he spots the difficulty, acknowledges the risk of misreading, and then shows how the text itself resolves the tension.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving to the Garden of Eden story, after Adam and Eve eat from the forbidden tree, God pronounces consequences for each of them. To the woman, God says in chapter three, verse sixteen, "And to your husband will be your desire, and he will rule over you." Rashi reads this as describing a specific psychological and social dynamic: the woman will desire her husband for intimacy, yet she will not have the boldness to express that desire openly — rather, the initiative will come from him. Rashi is doing something subtle here. He's reading the verse not just as a punishment but as a description of a relational imbalance, a shift in the dynamic between man and woman that results from the transgression in the garden. It's a comment that invites us to reflect on power, vulnerability, and the consequences of human choices.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to one of the most dramatic moments in the portion — the story of Cain and Abel. After God accepts Abel's offering but not Cain's, the Torah says in chapter four, verse eight, "And Cain said to Abel his brother," and then, in the Hebrew text, there's a striking gap — the verse never tells us what Cain actually said. It jumps straight to "and it came to pass when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against his brother Abel and killed him." Rashi explains that Cain picked a quarrel with Abel, seeking a pretext to fight with him. According to one midrashic tradition Rashi cites, they were arguing about how to divide the world between them. Rashi fills in the silence of the text, showing us that the Torah's gap is itself meaningful — the content of the argument almost doesn't matter. What matters is that Cain entered the conversation with violent intent. The missing words become a literary device, and Rashi draws our attention to it.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, at the very end of the portion, in chapter six, verse eight, the Torah tells us, "And Noah found grace in the eyes of the Lord." This single verse serves as a bridge between the total corruption of humanity and the story of the flood that will follow in next week's portion. Rashi's comment here is brief but important — he notes that this is a statement of divine favor, that amidst an entire generation marked for destruction, one man's righteousness caught God's attention. It sets up the tension that will drive the next portion: what does it mean to be righteous in a corrupt world, and is Noah's goodness absolute or only relative to his generation? Rashi will explore that question more fully next week, but even here, at the close of Bereshit, he plants the seed of that inquiry.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back to look at Rashi's commentary across this entire portion, several themes emerge. First, Rashi is deeply concerned with the purpose and structure of the text — not just what it says, but why it says it in this particular way and in this particular order. His very first comment asks why the Torah begins where it does, setting up a hermeneutical principle that nothing in the text is accidental. Second, Rashi pays extraordinary attention to the small details — a plural pronoun, a missing quotation, an unusual phrase — and shows how these tiny features carry enormous weight. Third, he's unafraid to bring in midrashic traditions to fill gaps in the narrative, but he does so carefully, always in service of explaining a genuine difficulty in the text. And finally, Rashi reads these ancient stories with a keen eye for human psychology — the shame of Eve, the rage of Cain, the humility modeled by God — reminding us that the Torah is not just telling history but teaching us about the human condition.
