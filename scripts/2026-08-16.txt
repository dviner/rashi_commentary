Parashah (Rashi): Ki Teitzei (Deuteronomy 21:10-25:19)
Date: 2026-08-16

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the brilliant and penetrating eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator. This week's portion is Ki Teitzei, found in Deuteronomy chapters twenty-one through twenty-five, and it is packed with laws, moral teachings, and some truly fascinating details that Rashi brings to life.

<break time="1.5s" />

Ki Teitzei contains more individual commandments than any other Torah portion, over seventy in total. It covers an extraordinary range of topics: the treatment of a captive woman in wartime, the rebellious son, returning lost property, building safety railings on rooftops, honest weights and measures, marriage and divorce, treatment of workers, care for the vulnerable, and much more. Because the portion moves so rapidly from one law to the next, Rashi is especially focused here on the connections between seemingly unrelated laws placed side by side. He asks again and again: why does the Torah put this law right next to that one? He also digs into unusual word choices, apparent contradictions, and the gap between what the text literally says and what the tradition understands it to mean. This is Rashi at his most precise, showing us that even in a long list of laws, every detail and every sequence carries meaning.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with one of the most striking passages in the entire Torah: the law of the beautiful captive. The Torah says that when a soldier goes to war and sees among the captives a beautiful woman, he may take her as a wife, but only after a specific process. She must let her nails grow long, remove her fine clothing, sit in his house and weep for her parents for a full month. Rashi asks the big question: why would the Torah permit this at all? His answer is startlingly honest. The Torah is speaking, he says, only in view of the evil inclination, the yetzer hara. God knows that if the Torah forbade this entirely, the soldier would take her anyway in violation of the law. So the Torah makes a concession to human weakness, but then immediately builds in a process designed to cool the soldier's passion. She lets her nails grow so she becomes less attractive. She removes her fine garments because, Rashi explains, the women of enemy nations would deliberately adorn themselves during wartime to tempt soldiers. She sits in his house, crying and disheveled, so that the novelty of her beauty wears off. And then Rashi makes a brilliant observation about the sequence of laws that follow. After the captive woman comes the law of the hated wife, and after that, the rebellious son. This juxtaposition, Rashi says, is deliberate: if a man marries through unbridled desire, he will come to hate his wife, and ultimately produce a wayward child. One bad decision cascades into another.

<break time="0.75s" />

Speaking of that rebellious son, the ben sorer u'moreh, Rashi offers a comment that has challenged readers for centuries. The Torah prescribes the death penalty for a son who is a glutton and a drunkard and refuses to obey his parents. Rashi explains that the Hebrew word sorer means one who deviates from the proper path, and moreh means one who is disobedient, rebellious. But here is the remarkable part. Rashi tells us that this son is not executed for what he has done so far. Rather, the Torah has fathomed his ultimate disposition. Left unchecked, he will exhaust his father's wealth, turn to robbery on the crossroads, and eventually commit crimes deserving death. The Torah says, Rashi explains, let him die while still innocent of those greater crimes, rather than die guilty of them. It is a stunning and deeply uncomfortable piece of reasoning, one that the Talmud itself struggles with, and it reveals how seriously Rashi takes the idea that the Torah sees the full trajectory of a life, not just a single moment.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to something gentler but equally profound: the commandment to send away the mother bird. The Torah says that if you come upon a bird's nest with the mother sitting on the eggs or young, you must send the mother away before taking the young. The Torah promises a reward for this, saying that it may be well with you and you may prolong your days. Rashi seizes on this and makes an argument from the lesser to the greater. If for such an easy commandment, one that costs you nothing financially, the Torah promises long life and well-being, how much greater must be the reward for commandments that are difficult to fulfill? And then Rashi does something characteristic: he connects this law to the ones that follow. If you fulfill the mitzvah of sending the mother bird, he says, you will merit building a new house, and then you'll need to fulfill the commandment of building a safety railing, and then you'll come to own a vineyard, and fields, and fine garments. One good deed leads to another, just as earlier, one sin led to another.

<break time="0.75s" />

Another comment worth exploring involves the laws of returning lost property. The Torah says you must not see your brother's ox or sheep going astray and hide yourself from them. Rashi notices the unusual grammar of the Hebrew phrase v'hit'alamta, and you shall hide yourself. He explains that this means closing one's eyes, pretending not to see. But the rabbis, Rashi adds, found a deeper teaching in the precise wording: there are indeed times when you may hide yourself, when you are permitted not to return the lost object, for instance if retrieving it would compromise your own dignity in a way the law does not require, or if other legal exemptions apply. Here we see Rashi's method beautifully: he gives us the plain meaning first, then opens a door to the broader legal discussion.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, consider Rashi's comment on why the Torah treats Ammon and Moab more harshly than Egypt and Edom. Egypt enslaved Israel and drowned their children, and Edom came out against them with the sword. Yet the Torah says not to utterly abhor an Egyptian or an Edomite. Meanwhile, the Ammonites and Moabites, who hired Balaam to curse Israel and tempted them into sin, are permanently excluded from marrying into the community. Rashi explains the reasoning with a powerful moral principle: one who causes another person to sin does him greater harm than one who kills him, because killing removes a person only from this world, while leading someone to sin removes him from both this world and the world to come. This is a remarkable ethical teaching embedded in what might look like a simple legal distinction between nations.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, several themes emerge across Rashi's commentary on Ki Teitzei. First, there is the idea of moral causation, that actions create chains of consequences. One sin leads to another sin, and one mitzvah leads to another mitzvah. Rashi sees this principle woven into the very structure of the Torah's text, in the way laws are placed beside each other. Second, Rashi consistently shows us a Torah that is deeply realistic about human nature, a Torah that makes concessions to weakness while building in safeguards, that judges people not only by their present actions but by the trajectories those actions set in motion. Third, Rashi reveals that even the most technical legal details carry profound moral weight. A law about returning lost animals teaches us about responsibility and integrity. A law about which nations may intermarry teaches us that spiritual harm is worse than physical harm. For Rashi, there is no such thing as a merely procedural commandment.
