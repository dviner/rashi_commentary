Parashah (Rashi): Pinchas (Numbers 25:10-30:1)
Date: 2026-06-28

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known simply as Rashi. This week we're looking at Parshat Pinchas, a rich and action-packed portion that takes us from an act of zealotry to the preparation for a new generation entering the Promised Land. Let's dive in and see what Rashi finds beneath the surface of these verses.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parshat Pinchas covers a lot of ground. It opens with God rewarding Pinchas for his bold act of slaying Zimri, an Israelite prince who publicly sinned with a Midianite woman. God grants Pinchas a covenant of peace and everlasting priesthood. From there, the portion moves into a full census of the Israelite people, counting the new generation that will enter the Land of Israel. We also encounter the compelling story of the daughters of Zelophehad, five women who petition Moses for the right to inherit their father's land since he died without sons. And the portion concludes with God's instruction to Moses to ascend a mountain where he will see the Promised Land but not enter it, followed by the appointment of Joshua as his successor and a detailed listing of the communal sacrificial offerings. Rashi, characteristically, zeroes in on puzzling word choices, genealogical details that seem unnecessary, and moments where the Torah's sequence of events hints at deeper connections between one passage and the next.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's start at the very beginning of the portion. The Torah introduces Pinchas with a seemingly redundant genealogy, calling him "Pinchas the son of Eleazar the son of Aaron the priest." We already know who Pinchas is, so why trace his lineage all the way back to Aaron? Rashi explains that the tribes of Israel were speaking disparagingly about Pinchas, saying, "Look at this grandson of Puti, whose maternal grandfather fattened calves for idol worship, and yet he dared to kill a prince of Israel!" The Torah therefore deliberately connects Pinchas to Aaron, the beloved high priest and pursuer of peace, to counter this slander and affirm that Pinchas acted from a noble lineage and with righteous intent.

<break time="0.75s" />

Staying with Pinchas, God rewards him with what the Torah calls a "covenant of everlasting priesthood." But wait — wasn't the priesthood already given to Aaron's descendants back in Exodus? Rashi addresses this with a remarkable clarification. The priesthood was indeed given to Aaron and to his sons who were anointed together with him, and to any children born to them after that anointing. But Pinchas had been born before the anointing took place. He had simply never become a priest. It was only now, through this act of zealotry, that Pinchas finally attained the status of priesthood. This is a striking detail, because it means that the man who carried out one of the boldest acts of religious devotion in the Torah did so not as a priest, but as an ordinary Israelite, and was elevated only afterward.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to the census. After the plague that killed twenty-four thousand Israelites, God commands Moses and Eleazar to count the people again. Why count them now? Rashi offers a beautiful parable. He compares God to a shepherd whose flock was attacked by wolves. After the attack, the shepherd counts the remaining sheep to know how many survived. There is tenderness in this image: God, like a caring shepherd, wants to know the state of His flock after devastation. Rashi also offers a second explanation — that when the Israelites left Egypt, they were entrusted to Moses by number, and now, as Moses approaches death, he must hand them back by number, like a steward returning a trust. Both readings transform a dry census into an act of love and accountability.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of the most fascinating details in the census involves the family names. Each clan is listed with a letter hei at the beginning and a yod at the end, so the family of Chanoch, for example, is called "HaChanochi." Why attach these particular letters? Rashi explains that the nations of the world mocked Israel, saying, "How can they trace their tribal descent? If the Egyptians had power over their bodies during slavery, they certainly had power over their wives." To refute this, God placed His own name upon the families. The letters hei and yod together form one of God's names, Yah. By embedding this divine name in each family name, God testifies that every Israelite truly belongs to the family they claim. Rashi supports this with a verse from Psalms: "The tribes of Yah are a testimony for Israel." It's a profound moment — God Himself serving as witness to the integrity and identity of His people.

<break time="0.75s" />

Let's move to the daughters of Zelophehad, one of the Torah's most remarkable episodes. Five sisters approach Moses and argue that their father, who died in the wilderness, deserves to have his name preserved through a land inheritance even though he left no sons. The Torah traces their lineage all the way back to Joseph, and Rashi asks why. The answer is deeply thematic: just as Joseph loved the Land of Israel and asked that his bones be carried there, so too his descendants, these five women, cherished the land and sought a stake in it. Rashi also notes that the women of that generation, unlike the men who wanted to return to Egypt, held the Promised Land dear. The men had said, "Let us appoint a chief and go back to Egypt," while the women said, "Give us a possession in the land." This, Rashi says, is precisely why the story of Zelophehad's daughters follows immediately after the census — to highlight the faithfulness of the women. And when God responds to their petition, He says, "The daughters of Zelophehad speak right." Rashi comments: happy is the person with whose words God agrees. Their moral clarity on the question of inheritance was so sharp that, as Rashi puts it, their eyes saw what Moses' eyes did not see.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, when Moses is told to appoint Joshua as his successor, the Torah says he should lay his hand upon Joshua. Rashi notes that God said "hand," singular, but Moses laid both hands upon Joshua, generously, even more than he was commanded. Moses made Joshua like a full and overflowing vessel, filling him with wisdom. And yet, Rashi also observes a limit. God told Moses to put "some of your glory" upon Joshua — not all of it. From this, the sages derived that Moses' face shone like the sun, while Joshua's face shone only like the moon. There is both generosity and poignancy here: Moses gave everything he could, but the next generation, however worthy, would carry a reflected light rather than the original radiance.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, several themes emerge from Rashi's commentary on this portion. One is the theme of identity and legitimacy — who belongs, who is worthy, who has earned their place. We see it in Pinchas's genealogy, in the divine letters embedded in the family names, and in the daughters of Zelophehad proving their claim. Another theme is transition — from one generation to the next, from Moses to Joshua, from the wilderness generation to the generation that will enter the land. Rashi is acutely sensitive to the human emotions in these transitions: Moses's hope that maybe the decree against him has been lifted, his generosity in empowering Joshua, and his care for the community even as he faces his own death. Throughout it all, Rashi shows us that the Torah's details are never accidental. A redundant genealogy, an unusual verb form, a shifted word order — each one is a door into deeper meaning, and Rashi holds out the key.
