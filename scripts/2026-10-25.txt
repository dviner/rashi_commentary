Parashah (Rashi): Vayera (Genesis 18:1-22:24)
Date: 2026-10-25

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, our weekly podcast where we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator whose genius for close reading continues to illuminate the text nearly a thousand years later. This week's portion is Vayera, which means "And He appeared," covering Genesis chapter eighteen through the end of chapter twenty-two. Let's dive in and see what Rashi notices that we might otherwise miss.

<break time="1.5s" />

Vayera is one of the most dramatic portions in the entire Torah. It opens with God appearing to Abraham as he sits at the entrance of his tent, recovering from his circumcision, and then three angelic visitors arrive with the news that Sarah will bear a son. From there, the narrative moves to the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah, Lot's rescue and the disturbing aftermath with his daughters, the birth of Isaac, the expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael, and finally the climactic Binding of Isaac, one of the most powerful scenes in all of scripture. Throughout this sweeping portion, Rashi does what he does best: he zeroes in on small textual details — an unexpected word, a missing detail, a grammatical oddity — and uses them to unlock layers of meaning we might never have seen on our own. In Vayera especially, Rashi is attentive to the gap between what characters say and what they mean, to the moral dimensions of hospitality and its violations, and to the ways the Torah conveys enormous human and spiritual complexity through its famously spare language.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's walk through some of Rashi's most striking comments on this portion. We'll start with a small but fascinating detail from the scene of Abraham's hospitality toward his three visitors. The Torah tells us in chapter eighteen, verse eight, that Abraham took butter and milk and the calf he had prepared and set it before his guests. Rashi notices something curious: Abraham had told Sarah to quickly prepare bread from fine flour, yet when the Torah lists what Abraham actually served, bread is conspicuously absent. Where did the bread go? Rashi explains, drawing on the Talmud in Bava Metzia, that Sarah became menstruant that very day — what the Torah later calls the "way of women" returning to her — and as a result, the dough she had prepared became ritually impure. Abraham, meticulous in his observance, would not serve impure bread to his guests. This is a remarkable comment because it simultaneously explains a textual gap, foreshadows the miraculous news about Sarah's fertility that is about to be announced, and reveals something about Abraham's character: even in the rush of generous hospitality, he maintains his spiritual standards.

<break time="0.75s" />

Staying with this same passage, Rashi pauses on the phrase in verse eleven that describes Sarah and Abraham as old, and then adds that "it had ceased to be for Sarah after the manner of women." The Hebrew uses two phrases here: "chadal lihyot," meaning it had ceased from her, and "orach kanashim," which Rashi clarifies means the way of menstruation. Rashi wants to make sure we understand that the Torah is speaking in concrete biological terms. Sarah's menstrual cycle had stopped — she was post-menopausal. This matters enormously because when, just moments later, the visitor announces that Sarah will have a son, the miracle is not merely that she is old but that her body has moved past the possibility of conception entirely. Rashi's precision here heightens our appreciation of just how extraordinary Isaac's birth will be.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to one of the darkest moments in the portion: the scene in Sodom. When the two angels arrive at Lot's home, the men of the city surround the house and demand that Lot bring out his guests. The Torah records their words in chapter nineteen, verse five: "Bring them out to us that we may know them." Rashi clarifies that the Hebrew word for "know" here, "neda," refers to homosexual assault, just as the same root is used elsewhere in the Torah to mean sexual relations, as in the phrase "who have not known a man." Rashi is not adding to the text so much as confirming what the context makes clear and what might otherwise be softened by translation. The men of Sodom are not simply being inhospitable — they are demanding to violate Lot's guests in the most degrading way possible. This comment helps us understand why the destruction of Sodom is so total and uncompromising: the city's sin is not merely rudeness but a systematic, communal assault on human dignity.

<break time="0.75s" />

After the destruction of Sodom, Lot's two daughters, believing the entire world has been destroyed, each lie with their father on successive nights in order to preserve humanity. The Torah states in chapter nineteen, verse thirty-six, that both daughters became pregnant. Rashi raises a biological question: typically, he notes, a woman does not become pregnant from her first act of intercourse. So how did both daughters conceive on their very first encounter? Rashi explains, drawing on the Midrash in Genesis Rabbah, that the daughters had deliberately broken their own hymens beforehand so that conception would be possible from the first union. This is a startling comment that underscores the daughters' determination and forethought. Rashi is not endorsing their actions, but he is acknowledging the Torah's implicit message that these women acted with deliberate purpose. The resulting children — Moab and Ammon — will become nations that play significant roles in Israel's future, and Rashi's comment hints that even in morally compromised circumstances, the unfolding of history proceeds with a strange, purposeful logic.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, we can see several themes running through Rashi's approach to Vayera. One is his relentless attention to gaps and silences in the text — the missing bread, the biological impossibility — as doorways into deeper meaning. Another is his willingness to engage with the text's most uncomfortable moments head-on, whether that means clarifying the nature of Sodom's sin or explaining the calculated actions of Lot's daughters. Rashi never flinches from the difficulty of the biblical narrative, and he trusts his readers to grapple with it honestly. Perhaps most importantly, Rashi reveals how every small detail in the Torah — a missing food item, an unusual phrase, a biological question — serves the larger story. Nothing is accidental. The Torah, in Rashi's hands, becomes a text where the smallest threads, when pulled, unravel into profound truths about human nature, divine justice, and the mysterious workings of providence.
