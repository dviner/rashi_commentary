Parashah (Rashi): Ki Tavo (Deuteronomy 26:1-29:8)
Date: 2026-08-23

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week's portion is Ki Tavo, found in Deuteronomy chapters twenty-six through twenty-nine, and it's a portion that takes us from beautiful rituals of gratitude to some of the most intense and sobering passages in all of Torah.

<break time="1.5s" />

Ki Tavo opens with two lovely ceremonial moments. First, there's the mitzvah of bikkurim, or first fruits, where a farmer brings the earliest produce of his harvest to the Temple and makes a declaration of gratitude, recounting Israel's journey from the days of the patriarchs through slavery in Egypt and into the Promised Land. Then comes vidui maaser, the tithe declaration, where a person affirms that they've properly distributed all their tithes. After these rituals, the portion shifts dramatically. Moses instructs the people about a ceremony at Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal, where blessings and curses will be proclaimed. And then comes the Tochacha, the long, harrowing litany of curses that will befall the nation if they abandon God's commandments. Rashi, characteristically, is drawn to the fine details in all of this. He asks why specific words appear, what legal rules are embedded in seemingly simple phrases, and how the structure of the text reveals layers of meaning that we might otherwise miss.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with the very opening of the portion. The Torah says, "And it shall be, when you come into the land and possess it and settle therein, then you shall take of the first of all the fruit of the ground." Rashi zeroes in on the phrase "and possess it and settle therein." Why does the Torah add these extra words? He explains that this teaches us the Israelites were not obligated to bring first fruits until they had both conquered and divided the land. The mitzvah doesn't kick in the moment they cross the Jordan. It requires full settlement. Then Rashi turns to the phrase "of the first," noting that it says "of the first" and not "all the first." Not every fruit qualifies. Only the seven species for which the Land of Israel is praised: wheat, barley, grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives, and dates. Rashi makes this connection through a textual parallel. The word "land" appears both here and in the verse listing those seven species in Deuteronomy chapter eight, linking the two passages. And even among those seven species, not every ripe fruit becomes bikkurim. Rashi explains that when a person goes into their field and sees the very first fig that has ripened, they tie a string around it and say, "This is bikkurim." It's a small, intimate gesture, one person marking one fruit, and it captures something profound about how gratitude begins with noticing.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at the declaration the farmer makes when bringing the first fruits. He begins with the famous words "Arami oved avi," which is often translated as "a wandering Aramean was my father." But Rashi reads it very differently. He translates it as "an Aramean destroyed my father," referring to Laban the Aramean, who pursued Jacob and wished to wipe out Jacob's entire family. Rashi says that even though Laban didn't succeed, God considers the intention of the nations as equivalent to the deed. So the Torah uses a past-tense verb, "destroyed," as if it actually happened. This reading transforms the declaration from a vague historical reference into a sharp theological statement. Gratitude, Rashi is showing us, is rooted in an awareness of the dangers we have survived, even the ones that never fully materialized.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving to the tithe declaration in chapter twenty-six, the person says before God, "I have removed the hallowed things from the house." Rashi asks what exactly are these "hallowed things"? He identifies them as the second tithe, which is called kodesh or holy, and also the fruit of a vineyard in its fourth year, which is likewise termed holy. The person is affirming that if they still had second tithes from the first two years of the sabbatical cycle sitting in their house, they have now properly brought them up to Jerusalem. Then the person continues, "I have also given to the Levite." Rashi says this refers to the first tithe. But then he catches the word "also," the Hebrew word "v'gam," and says the seemingly extra word comes to include terumah, the priestly portion, and first fruits as well. Rashi reveals an entire system of agricultural law encoded in what sounds like a simple personal statement. Every word carries legal weight.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to the curses. One of the most striking moments in Rashi's commentary comes when he compares the curses here in Ki Tavo with the earlier set of curses in Leviticus, in the portion called Bechukotai. The Torah says here that the heavens will become like copper and the earth like iron, but in Leviticus it says the reverse: heavens like iron and earth like copper. Rashi explains that there is actually a meaningful difference. In Leviticus, heavens like iron means no moisture at all comes from the sky, total drought, while earth like copper means the ground sweats and rots the fruit. That's worse. Here in Ki Tavo, heavens like copper means they do exude some moisture, even if not real rain, so at least there's no catastrophic drought, and earth like iron means the ground doesn't sweat, so the fruit doesn't rot. It's still terrible, Rashi says, because neither scenario produces food, but Moses, speaking in his own voice here rather than quoting God directly, was slightly milder in his formulation. Rashi is teaching us something remarkable about the nature of prophecy and about Moses's compassion even in delivering the harshest warnings.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, there's a devastating passage about the siege, where the Torah says that a tender and delicate person will eat the flesh of his own children because of the famine. Rashi explains that the words "tender" and "delicate" describe the same person, someone so refined that under normal circumstances he would be sickened by anything unpleasant. Yet hunger will so overwhelm him that he will refuse to share even this terrible food with his surviving children. Rashi's reading forces us to confront the full horror of the curse, that it doesn't just destroy bodies but inverts the most basic human instincts. The person who was once the most compassionate becomes, under unbearable pressure, the most cruel. It's a warning about what can happen when a society collapses, and Rashi doesn't look away from it.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, we can see several threads running through Rashi's approach to Ki Tavo. One is his attention to the precise legal architecture hidden within poetic and narrative language. The first-fruits ceremony and the tithe declaration are not just emotional expressions; they are carefully constructed legal acts, and every word matters. Another theme is Rashi's sensitivity to gradations, whether in the severity of curses, the levels of holiness in different tithes, or the distinction between God's voice and Moses's voice. And perhaps most powerfully, Rashi shows us that the Torah's vision of blessing and curse is not abstract. It's rooted in daily life, in a fig marked with a string, in rain that falls but not enough, in the human heart pushed beyond its limits. Rashi reads the Torah as a text that speaks simultaneously to the legal mind, the moral conscience, and the human heart.
