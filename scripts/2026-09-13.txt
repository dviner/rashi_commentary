Parashah (Rashi): Ha'Azinu (Deuteronomy 32:1-52)
Date: 2026-09-13

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator whose insights have illuminated Jewish learning for nearly a thousand years. This week's portion is Ha'Azinu, found in Deuteronomy chapter 32, and it contains one of the most powerful and poetic passages in the entire Torah. Let's see what Rashi helps us discover.

<break time="1.5s" />

Ha'Azinu is essentially Moses's farewell song. Standing at the edge of the Promised Land, knowing he will not cross over, Moses delivers a sweeping poem that calls heaven and earth as witnesses, praises God's justice and faithfulness, recounts how God found and nurtured Israel in the wilderness, and then warns of the consequences of forgetting that relationship. The song traces a dramatic arc — from God's tender care for His people, compared to an eagle caring for its young, to Israel's eventual prosperity, complacency, and descent into idolatry, and finally to the devastating consequences that follow. Because this portion is written as poetry rather than narrative, Rashi faces a distinctive set of challenges here. He's working with dense metaphors, unusual vocabulary, and imagery that needs unpacking. Many of his comments focus on clarifying what these poetic images actually mean, connecting them to specific moments in Jewish history, and revealing the emotional and theological layers beneath the surface of the language.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's walk through some of Rashi's most striking comments on this portion. The very first verse says, "Give ear, O heavens, and I will speak; and let the earth hear the words of my mouth." Rashi asks a question that might not immediately occur to us: why does Moses call on heaven and earth as his witnesses? His answer is deeply practical and deeply poignant. Moses says to himself, "I am flesh and blood. Tomorrow I will be dead. If the Israelites later claim they never accepted the covenant, who will be there to contradict them?" Heaven and earth, unlike Moses, endure forever — they are eternal witnesses. But Rashi adds a second, beautiful layer. These witnesses aren't just passive observers. If Israel acts worthily, the witnesses themselves deliver the reward — the heavens give rain, the earth yields its produce. And if Israel sins, the witnesses carry out the punishment — the heavens withhold rain, the earth withholds its bounty. In other words, the natural world itself is the mechanism of consequence. The witnesses are also the enforcers.

<break time="0.75s" />

A few verses later, we encounter a line that has become foundational in Jewish theology: "The Rock, His work is perfect, a God of faithfulness and without injustice, righteous and upright is He." Rashi draws out a remarkable idea here. On the phrase "a God of faithfulness," he explains that God is faithful to reward the righteous in the world to come — even though that reward may be delayed, God will ultimately keep His promise. Then on the words "and without injustice," Rashi makes a surprising move. He says this means God is without injustice even toward the wicked. God pays even the wicked for their good deeds — but He pays them in this world, not the next. So divine justice is precise and complete in both directions. No good deed goes uncompensated, and no person is treated unfairly, whether righteous or wicked. The system is meticulous.

<break time="0.75s" />

One of the most tender images in the entire Torah appears in verse eleven: "As an eagle stirs up its nest, hovers over its young, spreads its wings, takes them, bears them on its pinions." Rashi unpacks this metaphor with extraordinary care. He explains that the eagle, unlike other birds, does not enter its nest suddenly. Instead, it beats its wings above the nest, moving between branches, so that the young birds awaken gently and have time to gather their strength. And when the eagle needs to carry its young from place to place, it doesn't grasp them in its talons the way other birds do. Why? Because other birds fear the eagle above them and carry their young in their claws to protect them from attack from above. But the eagle has no predator above — it fears only the arrow from below. So it carries its young on its wings, saying, in Rashi's vivid paraphrase, "Better that the arrow pierce me than that it pierce my children." This, Rashi says, is how God carried Israel — shielding them with His own presence, placing Himself between Israel and danger, just as the angel of God moved between the Egyptian camp and the Israelite camp at the Red Sea to absorb the arrows and stones.

<break time="0.75s" />

Later, after describing God's abundant blessings — honey from the rock, oil from the flinty stone, the finest wheat and wine — the poem takes a devastating turn. Verse fifteen says, "Jeshurun grew fat and kicked — you grew fat, you grew thick, you became gross — and he forsook the God who made him." Rashi zeroes in on the word "kashita," which he says means "you became covered" — like a person so full of internal fat that his flesh lies in folds on the outside. The image is visceral and unflattering. Prosperity didn't just make Israel comfortable; it made them unrecognizable, encased in layers of excess that hid their true selves. And the very next phrase, "he lightly esteemed the Rock of his salvation," Rashi illustrates with a shocking image from Ezekiel: people standing in the Temple with their backs turned toward God's sanctuary. There can be no greater contempt, Rashi says, than this — not active rebellion, but casual dismissal, turning one's back on the source of everything.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, when God describes the punishment He will bring, He says in verse twenty-three, "I will heap evils upon them; I will use up My arrows against them." Rashi offers two readings of this verse, and the second one contains a hidden blessing. He reads it this way: "My arrows will come to an end, but they themselves will not come to an end." In other words, embedded within the very language of punishment is a promise of survival. God's arsenal of suffering is finite, but the Jewish people are not. Even in the darkest moment of the poem, Rashi finds an assurance of endurance. The curse, by its very wording, implies that Israel will outlast every catastrophe.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, we can see several powerful themes running through Rashi's commentary on Ha'Azinu. One is the precision of divine justice — God's system is not arbitrary but perfectly calibrated, rewarding and punishing with exacting fairness, even across the boundary between this world and the next. Another is the danger of prosperity: Rashi shows us again and again how abundance leads to complacency, how fullness leads to forgetfulness. But perhaps the deepest theme is God's tenderness even within severity. The eagle who shields its young with its own body, the arrows that run out before the people do — these are images of a God who punishes but never abandons. Rashi reads this poem not as a prediction of doom, but as what he calls "words of reproof" — a parent's warning born of love, meant to call a people back to awareness before it's too late.
