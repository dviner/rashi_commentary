Parashah (Rashi): Shmini Atzeret (Deuteronomy 14:22-16:17)
Date: 2026-09-27

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week's reading is called Shmini Atzeret, drawn from Deuteronomy chapters fourteen through sixteen, and it's packed with laws that touch on some of the most fundamental questions of how we live in community with one another.

<break time="1.5s" />

This portion covers a rich sweep of practical legislation. It begins with the laws of tithing, the obligation to set aside a portion of your agricultural produce. It then moves into the laws of the Sabbatical year, or Shemittah, when debts are released. A large section is devoted to caring for the poor, including detailed instructions about lending and generosity. We also find laws about Hebrew servants and how they must be treated and eventually freed. The portion concludes with the three pilgrimage festivals: Passover, Shavuot, and Sukkot, including detailed instructions about sacrifices, unleavened bread, and appearing before God. Rashi, as always, is drawn to the small textual puzzles: why are certain laws placed next to each other, what does a repeated word imply, and how do we reconcile verses that seem to contradict one another. In this portion especially, Rashi shows deep concern for the ethical dimensions of the law, reading moral psychology into individual word choices and grammatical forms.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with the very first verse of our portion: "You shall truly tithe all the increase of your seed." Rashi immediately asks a structural question. Why does the Torah place the tithing laws here, right after the preceding passage? His answer is striking. God is essentially saying to Israel: do not force Me to destroy your grain while it is still in the husk. If you fail to tithe properly, God will send the scorching east wind to blast the crops before they ripen. So the juxtaposition itself carries a warning: faithfulness in tithing is directly connected to the blessing of the harvest. Right from the start, Rashi frames tithing not as a mere tax, but as part of a covenantal relationship between God and the people.

<break time="0.75s" />

Next, let's look at one of Rashi's most psychologically rich comments, on the laws of giving to the poor. The Torah says, "You shall not harden your heart, nor shut your hand from your needy brother." Rashi breaks this into two distinct human tendencies. There are people, he says, who agonize internally about whether to give, going back and forth in painful deliberation. That is the hardened heart. Then there are people who actually reach out their hand, showing readiness to give, but then pull it back and close it. That is the shut hand. The Torah addresses both types because generosity can fail at either stage, in the mind or in the action. Rashi is reading the Torah as a keen observer of human nature, recognizing that resistance to giving is not one simple thing but a complex inner drama.

<break time="0.75s" />

Staying with the theme of charity, the Torah instructs us to provide for the poor person "sufficient for his need, in that which he lacks." Rashi takes this phrase and expands it dramatically. It means, he says, that if the person was once accustomed to riding a horse with a servant running before him, you are obligated to provide even that. The word "lo," meaning "for him," Rashi connects to the verse in Genesis where God says, "I will make a helper for him," teaching that you must even help the poor person find a spouse. This is a remarkable standard of generosity. Rashi is telling us that caring for the poor is not just about survival. It is about restoring a person's dignity, their sense of self, their place in the world.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to what seems like a contradiction in the text. In verse four, the Torah says, "There shall be no needy among you." But just a few verses later, in verse eleven, it says, "For the needy shall never cease out of the land." Rashi resolves this beautifully. When you do God's will, he explains, the needy will exist among others but not among you. But when you fail to do God's will, the needy will be among you. So both verses are true, but they describe different realities depending on the moral choices of the community. The apparent contradiction becomes a conditional promise, and Rashi transforms a textual puzzle into a profound ethical teaching.

<break time="0.75s" />

Moving to the laws of the Hebrew servant, the Torah commands that when you free a servant, "you shall furnish him liberally." The Hebrew word used here is ha'anek, and Rashi explains it refers to something conspicuous, like a neck ornament, something placed high on the body where it is easily seen. The idea is that your generosity should be visible and public, making it clear that you have treated this person well. And Rashi adds another layer: the Torah says to remember that you were once a slave in Egypt, and God loaded you with good things, from the spoils of Egypt and from the spoils at the Red Sea. Just as God gave to you abundantly and repeatedly, so you must give to the departing servant, loading him up not once but again and again.

<break time="0.75s" />

Finally, let's look at Rashi's comment on the festivals. When discussing Sukkot, the Feast of Tabernacles, the Torah says "you shall only be rejoicing." Rashi notes that on its plain level, this is not actually a command but a promise, an assurance that you will be joyful. But the Rabbis derived from this phrasing a legal obligation, that the night before the final day of the festival must also be included in the requirement of rejoicing. This is a beautiful example of how Rashi holds two readings simultaneously: the simple meaning of the text as a divine assurance of happiness, and the rabbinic reading that transforms joy itself into a sacred obligation.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back, what emerges from Rashi's commentary on this portion is a vision of a society built on radical responsibility toward others. Whether it is tithing, lending, freeing servants, or celebrating festivals, Rashi consistently emphasizes that every law has a human face. His comments on charity reveal his sensitivity to psychology, to the inner struggles we face when called to give. His reading of the servant laws insists on dignity and generosity that goes far beyond the minimum. And his treatment of the festivals reminds us that even joy is not simply a feeling but a commitment. Throughout, Rashi demonstrates his signature method: using small textual details, a repeated word, an unusual grammatical form, an apparent contradiction, to unlock large moral truths. For Rashi, the Torah's laws are never merely technical. They are windows into what it means to live in genuine relationship with God and with one another.
