Parashah (Rashi): Chayei Sara (Genesis 23:1-25:18)
Date: 2026-11-01

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, our weekly podcast where we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of Rashi, the great medieval commentator whose brilliance lies in noticing what most of us miss — the small cracks in the text where something doesn't quite fit, and then gently prying them open to reveal deeper meaning. This week's portion is Chayei Sara, which covers Genesis chapter 23 through chapter 25, verse 18, and as always, Rashi has some fascinating things to show us.

<break time="1.5s" />

Chayei Sara, which ironically means "the life of Sarah," actually begins with Sarah's death. Abraham mourns for her and then negotiates to purchase a burial plot — the Cave of Machpelah in Hebron — from the local Hittites. After that, the portion shifts to one of the Torah's longest and most beautifully told narratives: Abraham sends his trusted servant, typically identified as Eliezer, on a mission to find a wife for Isaac from Abraham's family back in Mesopotamia. The servant devises a test at a well, and Rebecca emerges as the one who passes it with extraordinary kindness, offering water not just to the servant but to all his camels. The portion then tells us about Isaac and Rebecca's marriage, Abraham's remarriage to a woman named Keturah, Abraham's death and burial, and finally a brief genealogy of Ishmael. In this portion, Rashi is particularly attuned to moments where the Torah seems to say more than it needs to, or where a word choice strikes him as redundant or unusual. He asks: if we already know something, why does the text repeat it? And when it uses a name we haven't seen before, who is this person really?

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's dive into some of Rashi's most thought-provoking comments on this portion. We'll start with one that might seem straightforward but actually reveals Rashi's incredibly close reading style. When the Torah introduces Rebecca at the well, it says she was a "betulah," a virgin, "and no man had known her." Now, if you're paying attention, that sounds redundant. If she's a virgin, then obviously no man had known her — so why does the Torah add that second phrase? Rashi picks up on this immediately. He explains that the word "betulah" refers specifically to physical virginity in the anatomical sense, while the additional phrase "and no man had known her" comes to tell us something further — that she had not been intimate in any other way either. Rashi notes that in the surrounding culture, women sometimes preserved technical virginity while engaging in other forms of intimacy. The Torah, Rashi says, is testifying that Rebecca was entirely pure, completely removed from such practices. This is Rashi at his most characteristic: he refuses to let a single seemingly extra word go unexplained, and in doing so, he paints a fuller portrait of Rebecca's exceptional character in a morally challenging environment.

<break time="0.75s" />

Now let's turn to a comment near the end of the portion that raises a wonderful question of identity. After Sarah's death and Isaac's marriage, the Torah tells us that Abraham took another wife whose name was Keturah. This name appears seemingly out of nowhere — we've never heard of this woman before. So Rashi asks the natural question: who is Keturah? And his answer is striking. Keturah, Rashi tells us, is actually Hagar — the same Hagar who was Sarah's handmaid, the mother of Ishmael, the woman Abraham had sent away years earlier. Rashi explains that she was given this new name, Keturah, for two reasons, both connected to the Hebrew root of the word. First, her deeds had become as beautiful and pleasing as "ketoret," which is the incense offered in the Temple — a symbol of something fragrant and elevated. Second, the name hints that she had "closed her opening," meaning she had remained faithful and had not been with anyone else during all the years since Abraham sent her away. This is a remarkable piece of commentary because it transforms our understanding of Hagar's story. She isn't just a secondary character who disappears from the narrative — she returns, changed, refined, worthy of a new name. Rashi is showing us that the Torah embeds character development even in a single word.

<break time="0.75s" />

What's especially beautiful about these two comments taken together — the one about Rebecca and the one about Keturah-Hagar — is how they create a kind of mirror. In both cases, Rashi is interested in the moral integrity of women, and in both cases, he finds that the Torah goes out of its way to affirm it. Rebecca, a young woman living among people whose values were questionable, maintained her purity completely. Hagar, sent away into the wilderness, could have lived however she wished — no one was watching — and yet she remained devoted. Rashi notices that the Torah doesn't just tell us facts about these women; it celebrates their inner strength through its word choices, and it's Rashi's job to make sure we don't miss that celebration.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from the individual comments, a clear theme emerges in Rashi's treatment of Chayei Sara: he is deeply interested in character — not just what people did, but who they were when no one was looking. Whether it's Rebecca's purity in a corrupt society or Hagar's quiet faithfulness during years of separation, Rashi uses the Torah's seemingly extra words and unusual names as windows into the inner lives of these figures. His method reminds us that in Torah study, redundancy is never really redundancy — every word carries weight, every repetition signals something the text wants us to notice. Rashi's approach in this portion also reveals his profound respect for the women of the Torah, whose virtue he highlights not through grand dramatic gestures but through the subtle testimony embedded in the text itself. For Rashi, the Torah is a document where nothing is accidental, and the careful reader is rewarded with layers of meaning that a casual glance would never uncover.
