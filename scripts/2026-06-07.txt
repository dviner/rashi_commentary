Parashah (Rashi): Sh'lach (Numbers 13:1-15:41)
Date: 2026-06-07

Welcome to Rashi in Brief, where each week we explore the Torah portion through the eyes of the great medieval commentator, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi. This week's portion is called Sh'lach, which means "send," and it's found in the Book of Numbers, chapters thirteen through fifteen. Let's dive in and see what Rashi reveals beneath the surface of this dramatic and heartbreaking story.

<break time="1.5s" />

Parshat Sh'lach tells one of the most consequential stories in the entire Torah. Moses sends twelve scouts, one from each tribe, into the Land of Israel to survey it before the Israelites enter. Ten of the twelve return with a terrifying report, describing powerful giants and fortified cities, and declaring the land unconquerable. Only two scouts, Joshua and Caleb, dissent, insisting that with God's help the people can succeed. But the people side with the ten, weeping through the night and begging to return to Egypt. God, deeply angered, decrees that this entire generation will wander the desert for forty years and never enter the Promised Land. Rashi focuses intensely on this portion, probing the motivations behind the spies' words, the subtle clues in the Torah's language that reveal hidden intentions, and the precise nature of the punishment. His commentary transforms what might seem like a straightforward narrative into a layered psychological and theological drama.

<break time="1.5s" />

Let's begin with the very first words of the portion. The Torah says, "Send for yourself men to scout the land." Rashi zeroes in on that phrase "for yourself," which in Hebrew is "lecha." Why doesn't God simply say "send men"? Rashi explains that God is saying, "I am not commanding you to do this. If you wish, send them." God had already told the Israelites the land was good, flowing with milk and honey. By adding "for yourself," God signals that this mission was the people's idea, not His. And Rashi goes further, citing the Talmud: God says, in effect, "I will give them the opportunity to stumble through the words of the spies, so that they will not inherit the land." This reading transforms the entire episode. It's not a neutral reconnaissance mission. From the very start, there is an undertone of divine testing and human failure.
<break time="0.75s" />
Rashi also asks why this story of the spies is placed immediately after the episode of Miriam being punished for speaking negatively about Moses. That juxtaposition, Rashi says, is deliberate. Miriam was struck with a skin disease for slandering her brother. These spies witnessed her punishment firsthand and should have learned a lesson about the destructive power of negative speech. Yet they went ahead and spoke slanderously about the land of Israel. The Torah places these stories side by side to underscore the gravity of the spies' sin: they had a vivid, recent warning right before their eyes, and they ignored it.
<break time="0.75s" />
Now let's look at something Rashi notices about Moses and Joshua. Before sending the scouts out, the Torah tells us that Moses changed Hoshea's name to Yehoshua, which is Joshua. Why? Rashi explains that the name Yehoshua is a combination of God's name and the word for "save." Moses was essentially offering a prayer embedded in a name: "May God save you from the evil counsel of the spies." Think about what this implies. Moses already suspected what might happen. He knew the group dynamic could turn dangerous, and he took a quiet, personal step to protect his protégé. It's a beautiful and poignant detail: the leader who cannot prevent the disaster tries to shield at least one person from it.
<break time="0.75s" />
One of Rashi's most psychologically astute comments concerns Caleb's visit to Hebron. The Torah uses a singular verb, "he came to Hebron," even though the spies were traveling as a group. Rashi explains that Caleb alone went to Hebron, where the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are buried, to prostrate himself at their graves and pray for strength not to be swept up in the other spies' evil counsel. This tells us that resisting peer pressure isn't automatic, even for a righteous person. Caleb knew he needed help. He actively sought out spiritual reinforcement. And Rashi connects this to Caleb's later reward: God promises him the very land of Hebron, the place where he went to pray.
<break time="0.75s" />
When the spies return, Rashi makes a brilliant observation about their rhetoric. The Torah says, "They went and they came to Moses." But we already know they returned — why mention their going? Rashi says the Torah is comparing their going with their coming. Just as their coming was with an evil plan, so too was their going. From the very moment they set out, most of them had already decided to bring back a bad report. And when they do report, they begin by saying, "The land truly flows with milk and honey." Why start with something positive? Rashi explains that any lie that doesn't begin with at least a grain of truth cannot stand. The spies were cunning. They established credibility with a true statement before pivoting to their fearful, exaggerated claims. It's a masterful observation about the anatomy of deception.
<break time="0.75s" />
Finally, let's look at Rashi's comment on the punishment. God decrees that the people will wander for forty years, one year for each day the spies spent scouting the land. But Rashi raises a practical problem: the land of Israel was enormous, roughly four hundred by four hundred parasangs, a Persian unit of distance. An average person walks about ten parasangs a day, so crossing just the width would take forty days, let alone traversing the whole country. How did the spies cover it in only forty days? Rashi answers that God miraculously shortened their journey, making them cover ground far more quickly than normal. Why? Because God already knew He would decree the punishment of one year per day, and He wanted the journey to last exactly forty days to set up that precise equation. The punishment was, in a sense, built into the mission from the beginning. Even the miracle of speed was part of the architecture of judgment.

<break time="1.5s" />

Stepping back from these individual comments, we can see several powerful themes running through Rashi's reading of this portion. First, there is the theme of speech and its consequences. From Miriam's slander to the spies' evil report to Caleb's clever rhetoric, words are the engine that drives this entire narrative, for good and for ill. Rashi is deeply attuned to the moral weight of language. Second, Rashi reveals the inner psychology of the characters: Moses's quiet worry for Joshua, Caleb's lonely pilgrimage to Hebron, the spies' calculated dishonesty. He turns a national catastrophe into an intimate human drama. Third, Rashi shows us that God's justice, while severe, operates with exacting precision, measure for measure, forty days becoming forty years, each detail of sin reflected in each detail of punishment. For Rashi, the Torah is never casual or accidental. Every word, every sequence, every grammatical oddity carries meaning, and our task as readers is to listen closely enough to hear it.
